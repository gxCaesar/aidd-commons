## 1. The budget is derived, not chosen

The nominal budget is twenty percent of the pool, but the reported fraction is
not twenty percent. The batch size is the floor of the pool count divided by
twenty, four rounds are run, and the acquired count is four batches.

In the primary assay a pool of 631 parents gives a batch of 31, an acquired
count of 124, and an actual budget fraction of 0.196513470681458. In the
replication assay a pool of 130 gives a batch of 6, an acquired count of 24, and
a fraction of 0.18461538461538463. Integer rounding, not a design choice, is why
neither equals the nominal target, and the contract says so rather than
reporting the round number.

The checker recomputes all of it, including that the three partitions sum to the
902 and 170 parents inherited from the frozen ChEMBL contract. A budget study
whose partition sizes drift from the dataset it claims to replay is comparing
policies on a different problem.

## 2. What a policy sees, and what a seed is not

The diversity policy is label-blind. The contract states that its selector must
not receive the initial, pool or evaluation labels, which is what makes its
selections a property of the chemistry rather than of the answer.

The five random seeds are the part most often misread. The protocol calls them
fixed policy instances, not independent assays or samples. That wording is doing
real work. In the replication assay their recovery AUCs run from
0.009615384615384616 to 0.10576923076923077, an eleven-fold spread across what
looks like the same baseline. It is tempting to average them and quote the
spread as an error bar.

That would claim something the design cannot support, and the exercise below
asks you to say exactly what.

## 3. Fit, lock, then score

The prediction contract fixes an order rather than a formula. At each checkpoint
the model is fitted only on the initial parents plus everything acquired through
that checkpoint, never on evaluation parents. Every acquisition order is
finalised and every evaluation prediction vector is locked **before** the scorer
touches an evaluation label. Then, and only then, the error is scored. The
contract closes with the rule that evaluation outcomes cannot change a policy, a
seed, a partition, a feature, an alpha, a batch or any future prediction vector.

You can see the ordering in the output rather than take it on trust. Every
policy reports the identical error at checkpoint zero, because at that point no
policy has acquired anything and all of them are fitted on the same initial set.
A checker confirms it. If those numbers ever differed, something had leaked
backwards into round zero.

## 4. The result that went against the method

In both assays the diversity policy finished below every one of the five random
instances. In the replication assay it recovered nothing at all across the whole
budget, an AUC of 0.0 against random instances reaching 0.10576923076923077.

The command prints that comparison as a sentence rather than leaving it to be
inferred from a table, and the summary keeps it. A pool of 130 parents with a
target count of 13 is a small problem, and the honest reading is that this
campaign does not show diversity selection working here. It is not evidence that
diversity selection never works.

## Independent exercise

A colleague proposes reporting "the random baseline" for the replication assay
as the mean of the five seed AUCs, with their spread from
0.009615384615384616 to 0.10576923076923077 shown as an error bar. Say what that
presentation would claim, why the protocol's own wording rules it out, and what
the target count of 13 tells you about the spread before any statistics are
considered.

<details data-worked-answer>
<summary>Worked answer — the claim, the wording and the granularity</summary>

**What it would claim.** An error bar invites the reader to treat the interval as
sampling uncertainty about a population, and therefore to ask whether the
diversity policy falls outside it. That is a significance reading. It would say
that if the experiment were repeated, the random baseline would land in this
range.

**Why the wording rules it out.** The protocol calls the seeds fixed policy
instances, not independent assays or samples. Repeating the experiment does not
resample anything: the assay, the partition, the pool and the targets are all
frozen and shared. Changing a seed changes which permutation of one fixed pool a
policy walks. The spread measures how much that one choice matters on this one
dataset, which is a fact about the policy, not an uncertainty about a
population.

**What the target count tells you.** Recovery is the count of acquired targets
divided by the target count, which here is 13. One additional target moves
recovery by one thirteenth, about 0.077. The full observed spread of the five
seeds is roughly one such step. Before any statistical argument, the granularity
of the measurement already accounts for most of the range, which is another way
of seeing that an interval built from it would be describing arithmetic rather
than evidence.

A complete answer notices that reporting all five instances individually, as the
summary does, communicates the same information without the claim, and that the
right response to a spread this coarse is a larger target set rather than a
different formula.
