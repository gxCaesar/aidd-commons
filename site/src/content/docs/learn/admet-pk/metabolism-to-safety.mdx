## Learning goals and prerequisites

You will identify victim and perpetrator DDI roles, distinguish a toxicity
endpoint from transferable safety evidence, and make a transparent multi-parameter
ADMET decision. Read [Exposure, clearance, and PK/PD](/learn/admet-pk/exposure-clearance-pkpd/)
and [ADMET evaluation](/learn/admet-pk/admet-evaluation/) first. This is a
conceptual, hand-calculation lesson: it supplies neither dosing advice nor a
laboratory protocol.

## Metabolism changes the exposure question

Metabolism may convert a parent compound to metabolites, altering parent and
metabolite concentration over time. Enzymes and transporters can therefore
matter as a candidate's **victim** (another agent changes its exposure) or its
**perpetrator** (it changes another agent's exposure). These are roles in a
specified interaction, not inherent labels. Current ICH M12 guidance concerns
enzyme- and transporter-mediated pharmacokinetic DDI potential and harmonizes
in-vitro and clinical evaluation concepts [FDA M12](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/m12-drug-interaction-studies-questions-and-answers).

`CL = rate of elimination / concentration` describes clearance at the stated
moment and model; its units are volume/time. For a first-pass conceptual model,
if bioavailability `F`, dose `D`, and clearance `CL` are fixed, exposure scales
as `AUC ∝ F·D / CL`. This relation is a useful directional model, not a clinical
prediction. A metabolic inhibition signal can suggest lower clearance and higher
parent exposure, but it cannot determine the magnitude in people without the
rest of the model, concentrations, and validation.

## Worked case: does inhibiting one enzyme predict inhibiting another?

"This compound inhibits CYP" is not yet a claim you can check: it names no
enzyme, no assay, and no document. The [CYP assay-system
lab](/labs/cyp-assay-system/) reads live ChEMBL 37 IC50 records for two
different cytochrome P450 enzymes, CYP3A4 and CYP2D6, and asks a narrower
question: for real compounds with an admitted record against **both**
enzymes, does apparent potency against one predict potency against the
other? No model is fitted; every number below is a count, a median, or a
rank correlation over already-published measurements, computed on
2026-09-12 against the live ChEMBL 37 release dated 2026-05-01.

| | CYP3A4 (`CHEMBL340`) | CYP2D6 (`CHEMBL289`) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Admitted parent molecules | 5,251 | 2,781 |

Of those, 1,195 parents have an admitted record against both enzymes; 1,186
of those share at least one source document across the two targets; 1,015
are "clean" single pairs, meaning exactly one admitted activity per enzyme
from the same document, so neither side is a cross-study median. Spearman
rho over the full overlap is 0.2981 (p=6.08e-26); over the clean-pair
subset, 0.3038 (p=4.10e-23). The two estimates agree with each other as a
robustness check, and both sit far from either 0 or 1: apparent CYP3A4
potency is a real but weak positive signal for CYP2D6 potency here, not a
strong predictor and not independent noise either.

Two named examples from the clean-pair subset show what that weak
correlation hides. Albaconazole (`CHEMBL298817`) is markedly CYP3A4-
selective: pIC50 7.40 against CYP3A4 versus 4.50 against CYP2D6, in the same
document. Quinidine (`CHEMBL1294`) is not a clean pair at all, and that
absence is itself informative: it carries exactly one admitted CYP3A4
record (pIC50 4.55) against 32 independent CYP2D6 records spanning a pIC50
range of 4.1, more than a 10,000-fold range in reported potency, with a
median pIC50 of 7.37. A single number for "quinidine and CYP" would have
to choose among 33 real measurements before it named anything.

**Independent exercise:** A safety summary states "Compound Z inhibits
CYP450, IC50 = 50 nM." Using only the assay-system distinction this case
draws, name three fields this sentence omits that would be required before
the claim could be checked against a ChEMBL-style record, and say what each
omission risks.

<details data-worked-answer>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

The sentence omits which CYP enzyme was tested (CYP3A4 and CYP2D6 potency
correlate only weakly here, so a value for one is not a value for the
other), which assay generated the number (a single target's records here
span many independent assays and documents, and quinidine's 32 CYP2D6
records range over a pIC50 window of 4.1), and which document reported it
(without a shared document, two numbers for the same compound cannot be
treated as a same-study "clean pair" the way this lab defines one). Without
all three, "Compound Z inhibits CYP450" cannot be located in, or checked
against, a real record like the ones this case reads.

</details>

## Toxicity endpoints have a system and an inference limit

A toxicity endpoint is not “safe” or “unsafe”; it is a measurement on a named
system, exposure window, readout, and comparison. A viability change in a cell
model may be useful for ranking within its range. It does not prove organ
toxicity, absence of toxicity, exposure relevance, or clinical transfer. The
right transport question is: does the new context preserve the biological
mechanism, exposure relation, and decision endpoint needed by the claim? If not,
report a context-specific association, not a generalized safety conclusion.

Make the denominator explicit. If `S_test` and `S_vehicle` are background-corrected
signals where high means viable cells, then `% viability = 100·S_test/S_vehicle`.
If the system instead reports damage or a different normalization, this formula
may be wrong. Define a constructed safety separation as `M = C_risk / C_effect`,
where `C_effect` is the stated concentration for the desired endpoint and
`C_risk` is the stated concentration for the risk endpoint under comparable
conditions. `M` is dimensionless only when those threshold concentrations are
comparable; a larger `M` means greater separation in this limited model. An
in-vitro `M` is a prioritization ratio, not a clinical therapeutic index.

## Multi-parameter optimization exposes trade-offs

For each candidate, retain the raw endpoint, unit, system, date or batch,
missingness, and the rule that maps it to a decision. A hard-gate policy is easy
to audit: a candidate advances only when each declared minimum is met. A soft
rank may follow among candidates meeting gates, but cannot rescue a failure with
an attractive average. This matters because potency, solubility, clearance,
permeability, DDI signals, and model-specific safety signals can move in opposite
directions.

## Worked case: a constructed Pareto decision

All values below are **constructed for teaching**. Before inspection, a team
sets four minimum gates: cellular `pIC50 ≥ 6.00`; solubility proxy `≥ 20` units;
clearance proxy `≤ 35` units (lower is preferred); and constructed viability at
the comparison concentration `≥ 75%`. An additional DDI flag is not a pass/fail
number: it requires an explanation and a next action.

S fails the solubility gate and T fails the clearance gate. R meets the four
listed gates but is not declared “safe”: it is a conditional survivor with an
unresolved transporter hypothesis. Suppose a simplified exposure model gives R
`AUC_R ∝ 1/31` and T `AUC_T ∝ 1/39` for equal `F·D`; the ratio is
`AUC_R/AUC_T = 39/31 = 1.26`. This constructed 1.26-fold direction depends on
the unrealistic equalities and says nothing about human exposure. It merely
explains why the clearance field cannot be ignored while comparing R and T.

The next decision for R is an appropriately scoped mechanistic follow-up or a
more relevant exposure-risk model, predeclared before observing that result. It
is not to average “good” columns against the DDI flag. A missing assay remains
missing, rather than receiving a favourable default.

## Alternatives, limitations, and independent exercise

Pareto selection can preserve R and a different candidate when neither dominates
the other; it is especially honest when weights have no defensible meaning. A
weighted desirability function may aid resource allocation but needs fixed
normalization and weights. Both approaches fail when endpoint systems differ,
when concentration is not comparable to expected exposure, or when a safety
mechanism is unknown. The FDA's DDI framework is risk-oriented; it does not turn
an in-vitro result into a clinical label [FDA in-vitro DDI guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/134582/download).

**Handworked exercise (not runnable or empirical):** Apply the same constructed
gates to U: `pIC50 5.95`, solubility `32`, clearance `30`, viability `91%`; and
V: `pIC50 6.20`, solubility `22`, clearance `34`, viability `72%`. Which gate
does each fail? If a third candidate W has `CL = 24` versus R's `31` under equal
`F·D`, compute `AUC_W/AUC_R`. State why that ratio is not a clinical safety
claim, and name the relevant DDI role if W changes the exposure of another drug.

<details data-worked-answer>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

U fails the potency gate (`5.95 < 6.00`); V fails the viability gate
(`72% < 75%`). With the stated simplifying assumptions,
`AUC_W/AUC_R = 31/24 = 1.29`. The ratio ignores bioavailability, time course,
metabolites, species, concentrations, and translation, so it is not a clinical
safety claim. If W changes another drug's exposure, W is the perpetrator and the
other drug is the victim in that specified interaction.

</details>

## Separate the levels yourself

The [safety evidence lab](/labs/safety-evidence/) computes structural alerts from
structures while storing only findings that required an experiment, so the two can
never be printed as one column. Seven of twelve compounds alert and five of those
were never measured at all, the reassuring direction fails outright with positive
findings on compounds that alert on nothing, and one of twelve carries any
organism-level evidence.
