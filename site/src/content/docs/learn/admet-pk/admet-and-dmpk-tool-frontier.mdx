## Learning goals and prerequisites

The track already teaches what exposure, metabolism, and safety mean. This
reading moves one step along the same boundary, to the open tooling that turns
those concepts into predictions, and to what each released artefact actually
makes available. Read [From metabolism to safety decisions](/learn/admet-pk/metabolism-to-safety/)
and [Choosing ADMET and PK resources](/learn/admet-pk/admet-resource-guide/)
first. Neither of them is repeated here, and nothing below supplies dosing
advice, a regulatory interpretation, or a laboratory protocol.

After this lesson you should be able to:

1. state the input and output contract of each tool in the family;
2. say what the records do and do not establish about representation,
   objective, training, and inference;
3. read the seven openness dimensions of a record and say which one a given
   claim depends on;
4. separate a licence question from an access question and from a scientific
   question;
5. say why a published endpoint count does not establish that a prediction was
   made on a compound like yours; and
6. say what a matched comparison would need before two of these tools could be
   placed next to each other.

The work is reading. No tool in this lesson was installed, run, or reproduced,
and no prediction was produced. The four software and platform records were
last verified on 2026-10-21, the Therapeutics Data Commons record on
2026-09-04, and the Contrastive KERMT record on 2026-10-23. Every number on
this page is a number those records state.

## Task and input-output contract

Six artefacts sit in this family. They differ in what they take in, what they
return, and what they leave to the reader.

| Artefact | What it operates | Input | Output |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ADMET-AI | A small molecule and a set of ADME endpoints | A molecule structure | Predicted ADME endpoint values |
| ADMETlab | A small molecule and a catalogue of ADME and toxicity endpoints | A molecule structure | Predicted physicochemical, ADME and toxicity values, and toxicophore rule calls |
| Polaris | A dataset, a benchmark split and a method | A method and a benchmark | A score against a split that somebody else curated |
| Therapeutics Data Commons | Datasets, tasks and benchmarks | A dataset name | The data and the split, not a prediction |
| OpenADMET Models | A dataset, a representation and a training routine | A dataset and a training routine | A predictor that the user still has to evaluate |
| Contrastive KERMT | A pretrained molecular representation for ADMET and DMPK tasks | Molecules and the user's labelled endpoint data for fine-tuning | A backbone to fine-tune, not a ready-made endpoint prediction |

[ADMET-AI](https://github.com/swansonk14/admet_ai) is a Python package, a
command-line tool, and a hosted web service that predicts ADME endpoints with
Chemprop models trained on Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC) datasets. A
separate archive on Zenodo holds the version 1 Chemprop and Chemprop-RDKit
models, the TDC ADMET data, RDKit features, and five-split benchmark files.

[ADMETlab](https://admetlab3.scbdd.com/) is a web platform that predicts
physicochemical, ADME, and toxicity properties of small molecules. The hosted
ADMETlab 3.0 service is built on DMPNN models, which the site describes as the
Directed Message Passing Neural Network framework. A separate repository
distributes the earlier model archives described in the 2018 paper together with
the code needed to run them locally, and that repository's README does not
state that it implements the 3.0 release served by the site. The local
archives and the service are therefore two different artefacts, and the local
ones are not the service's models.

[Polaris](https://github.com/polaris-hub/polaris) is a hosted platform for
sharing drug-discovery datasets and benchmarks, with a Python client library
that downloads datasets, loads benchmark splits, and evaluates a method against
a benchmark.

A related dataset, [ADME-Fang-v1](https://polarishub.io/datasets/biogen/adme-fang-v1), is Biogen's curated release of laboratory ADME measurements from the [Fang et al. industrial validation study](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jcim.3c00160). The dataset page reports 3,521 datapoints in its summary and 3,516 molecules after curation without reconciling those figures. Its measurements came from one laboratory group under consistent conditions over 20 months, not from multiple sites. The page states no train, validation or test partition, and the published [curation notebook](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/polaris-hub/polaris-recipes/main/org-Biogen/fang2023_ADME/01_polaris_adme-fang_data_curation.ipynb) configures molecule curation, distribution inspection, outlier detection and stereoisomer activity-shift detection without defining a benchmark split. The dataset is openly downloadable under its stated CC BY 4.0 licence, but no opened page declares it a training release or a split for a specific model, so its training-data dimension remains unverified.

[Therapeutics Data Commons](https://tdcommons.ai/) is the official collection
of therapeutic machine-learning tasks, datasets, and benchmarks. It supplies
the material rather than a prediction, and it is the training source named in
ADMET-AI's README.

[OpenADMET Models](https://github.com/OpenADMET/openadmet-models) is a
collection of model implementations and training routines for building, testing,
and prototyping ADMET predictors.

[Contrastive KERMT](/atlas/model-kermt/) is NVIDIA's pretrained graph-transformer
backbone for molecular representation learning. Its [model card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/NV-KERMT-70M-v2)
says users are expected to fine-tune it on their own labelled datasets for
specific ADMET endpoints. The record carries no ADMET prediction from this model.

The boundary that separates them is simple to state and easy to lose. A
hosted service returns a value for your molecule. A benchmark returns a score
for your method on someone else's split. A toolkit supplies routines for
building a predictor. A dataset returns rows. A pretrained backbone supplies
representations that still need endpoint-specific fine-tuning. Only a service's
returned value here is a prediction about a compound, and no prediction is a
measurement.

## Representation, objective, training and inference

The older tool records give a thin answer to this question. Contrastive KERMT's
model record describes its representation and pretraining objective more directly.

**Representation.** Contrastive KERMT is a graph transformer with 70.6 million
parameters, derived from GROVER and KERMT. The other explicit representation
statement is a difference between two releases of the same package, because
the ADMET-AI record says that version 2 was retrained from scratch with
Chemprop v2 and without RDKit fingerprints. The version 1 models
in the archive were built with those fingerprints, and the archive ships the
corresponding features. The ADMETlab record names a framework rather than a
representation, and the OpenADMET Models record marks architecture as
available without describing it inside the record. The TDC record describes no
representation at all.

**Objective.** Contrastive KERMT was pretrained without labels on one joint
objective combining SMILES reconstruction, in-batch contrastive discrimination,
and chemistry-specific self-supervision. The other records name endpoints but
do not state a training objective. An endpoint is a target rather than an
objective; those records do not say how a loss was formed.

**Training.** The training stories are the most concrete part of the family, and
they differ in kind. ADMET-AI's README names the TDC ADMET datasets as the
training source, its archive ships that data with the split files, and its
package ships a scripts directory that the record treats as the evidence for
training code, data recipe, and evaluation code. ADMETlab states that its
models are trained on an internal database of 0.37M entries spanning 77
endpoints, and it publishes neither that database, the training code, nor the
model definitions. OpenADMET Models supplies the training routine and leaves
the data recipe, the training data, and the endpoint-specific provenance
unestablished. Contrastive KERMT's card describes 100 epochs of unsupervised
pretraining over about 11.1 million unique canonical SMILES. The pool starts
with ZINC15 and ChEMBL and adds Biogen ADMET, ExpansionRX, and ChEMBL-MT. It was
deduplicated by canonical SMILES and scaffold-balanced for the train and
validation split. The record did not inspect the underlying corpora, and the
pretraining used no dedicated testing dataset.

**Inference.** Here the family separates cleanly. ADMET-AI gives the reader a
package, a command-line tool, and a hosted service. OpenADMET Models gives
inference code and a training routine. Polaris gives evaluation code that
scores a method against a split. ADMETlab gives a hosted service, and the
local archives belong to an earlier release whose own README does not claim the
3.0 behaviour. Contrastive KERMT supplies code and weights for a pretrained
backbone rather than a ready-made ADMET predictor. The decoder's recommended
maximum SMILES length is 512 tokens; longer structures are to be truncated or
omitted. The card describes Linux with NVIDIA GPUs as the deployment target.

One consequence runs through all of it. The ADMET-AI record states that the
version 2 predictions do not exactly match the version 1 models in the archive
or in the published paper, so a reader who installs the package and a reader
who downloads the archive are running two different model generations under two
different licences.

## Openness and licence posture

Every record carries the same seven openness dimensions, and each dimension
carries its own evidence. A cell that reads unverified is a statement that no
claim is made, not a statement that the artefact is closed. A cell that reads
not applicable is a statement that the dimension does not apply to that
artefact at all.

| Dimension | [ADMET-AI](/atlas/tool-admet-ai/) | [ADMETlab](/atlas/tool-admetlab/) | [Polaris](/atlas/tool-polaris/) | [TDC](/atlas/benchmark-tdc/) | [OpenADMET Models](/atlas/tool-openadmet-models/) | [Contrastive KERMT](/atlas/model-kermt/) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Access level | open source | source available | open source | unverified | open source | open source |
| Inference code | available | available | available | unverified | available | available |
| Training code | available | unverified | not applicable | unverified | available | available |
| Weights | available | available | unverified | unverified | unverified | available |
| Architecture | available | unverified | unverified | unverified | available | available |
| Data recipe | available | unverified | unverified | unverified | unverified | available |
| Training data | available | unverified | unverified | unverified | unverified | available |
| Evaluation code | available | unverified | available | unverified | unverified | unverified |

ADMET-AI is the only record in the family with all seven dimensions
established. The MIT licence file in the repository covers the package
source code, the version 2 Chemprop checkpoints bundled with the package, and
the packaged CSV data files, and it states no further conditions. A second
licence, on the Zenodo record, covers the deposited data and model archives,
which hold the version 1 Chemprop and Chemprop-RDKit models, the TDC ADMET
data, the RDKit features, and the benchmark-group splits, and it requires
attribution to the record creators. The bundled version 2 checkpoints carry no
licence statement beyond the repository's MIT file, and the archive's licence
does not extend to them.

Contrastive KERMT's card states that the source code is under Apache 2.0 and
the weights under the NVIDIA Open Model License. It says the model is ready for
commercial or non-commercial use. The record did not inspect the linked licence
terms page, so the detailed terms for the weights are not established here.
Its evaluation-code dimension remains unverified, despite the other six
dimensions being marked available.

The TDC column is the one to read twice. That record leaves its status, its
access level, and all seven openness dimensions unverified, and it lists no
licence at all. It is also the training source named in ADMET-AI's README, so
the widest-openness record in the family leans on a record that claims
nothing. The two facts are not in conflict, because ADMET-AI's own record
establishes the training data separately, on the strength of the archive that
ships it. What the TDC record leaves open is the collection's own access and
licence position, which is a different question from the archive's.

Polaris answers the same question in the other direction. Its client library is
open source and its training-code dimension is not applicable, because a client
that downloads a split and scores a method does not train models. The weights
dimension stays unverified because the Hub's model listing shows six models
with no weight or checkpoint download and no model-specific licence.

The licence questions in this family are not one question, and two of them fail
in ways a reader has to know about.

The ADMETlab repository carries a GNU Affero General Public License v3 in its
LICENSE file, which is the only licence file in that repository, and the README
of the same repository names a New BSD licence for the software and its
associated data and documents. The record keeps both statements and treats
neither as controlling, so the effective terms for reuse are unverified. The
conflict is recorded rather than settled, and a reader has to ask the authors
which statement governs.

The 3.0 service is a separate question again, and it is the harder one. The
site states no licence for the prediction service, and its only LICENSE link
points to a GitHub repository path that does not exist and returns HTTP 404.
The terms under which a service's output may be kept, published, or
redistributed are therefore unverified, and no amount of reading the
repository's licence file answers them.

The OpenADMET Models licence has the same shape of problem. The file states
Apache License, Version 2.0 with sections 1 to 9 complete and the standard
Appendix boilerplate truncated, and the Appendix carries the project notice
"Copyright 2025 Open Molecular Software Foundation" where the standard notice
would normally sit. The README of the same repository states that the library
is made available under the MIT open source licence. The record follows the
licence file and preserves the conflict. That file's scope covers the
repository source code and does not extend to any dataset, model, or dependency
the project uses.

Polaris separates the two cleanly, which makes it the clearest case in the
family. The Apache-2.0 licence covers the source code of the Python client
distributed on PyPI as polaris-lib and on conda-forge as polaris, and it covers
that client code only. It grants no right to use, redistribute, or re-host the
hosted Polaris Platform or the datasets, benchmarks, and models that the
platform serves. The hosted service is operated by Valence Labs under the
Polaris Platform Agreement, last updated on 3 April 2024, which licenses the
documentation on a revocable, non-exclusive, non-transferable, and
non-sublicensable basis limited to using the platform, prohibits
sub-licensing, selling, renting, lending, leasing, or distributing the platform
and its intellectual property, and prohibits use for building a similar or
competitive product or service. A dataset uploaded by another user is governed
by the licence stated with that dataset, which without one defaults to a right
to use, display, and perform it through the platform and to reproduce it on the
platform as permitted by Valence Labs.

## The comparison the records do not support

The strongest relevant comparison under a matched protocol is not available in
these records, and the reason is not that the tools are hard to compare. It is
that the comparison was never specified.

ADMET-AI's archive ships five-split benchmark files, and its README names the
TDC ADMET datasets as the training source. The opened pages do not document
the curation, filtering, or split rules used to build the released benchmark
splits. A split without its construction rules cannot be re-derived, and two
scores on two such splits are not a comparison.

Polaris supplies the machinery for a matched comparison. Its client library
loads benchmark splits and evaluates a method against a benchmark, and the Hub
lists datasets, benchmarks, competitions, and models. The Hub's own curation
guidance is incomplete, because the small-molecule guidelines page marks the
Data Curation paper as Planned and the Splitting Methods paper as In Progress,
so no published curation or split rule is available. The models page lists six
models but shows no weight or checkpoint download and no model-specific
licence, so the weights dimension stays unverified and those models cannot be
run from the Hub as it stands.

Inside ADMET-AI there is one comparison the record makes on its own. The
version 2 models do not exactly match the version 1 models in the archive or in
the published paper. That is a measured statement about a released artefact, and
it is the only internal comparison the family offers.

Contrastive KERMT's card reports no numerical benchmark, and its unsupervised
pretraining used no dedicated testing dataset. Its scaffold-balanced train and
validation split is a pretraining split, not an endpoint evaluation. The record
therefore supplies neither an ADMET prediction nor a ranking against ADMET-AI,
ADMETlab, Polaris, or Therapeutics Data Commons.

A reader who wants a comparison has to supply the protocol that the records do
not. It would need one split with its construction rules published, one
endpoint definition with units, one metric, the same weights on both sides, and
a stated domain of applicability. A ranking of the family without that is an
invention rather than a reading.

## Entities, splits, metrics and resources

The entities underneath these tools are the entities of the track. A training
compound, a labelled endpoint with a unit, a split, a metric, and a decision
threshold are what a prediction is about.

ADMET-AI's record supplies model checkpoints, data, and split files. ADMETlab's
record supplies an internal database of 0.37M entries spanning 77 endpoints
that it does not publish. Polaris's record supplies datasets and benchmarks
governed by third-party licences. The TDC record supplies a collection whose
access, licence, and seven openness dimensions are all unverified. Contrastive
KERMT's record names an unlabelled pretraining pool of about 11.1 million unique
canonical SMILES, but no endpoint labels or predictions from its backbone.
That is the entity picture a reader has to work with.

ADMET-AI names five-split benchmark files without their construction rules or
an attached metric. Contrastive KERMT names a scaffold-balanced train and
validation split for label-free pretraining, with no dedicated test set and no
numerical benchmark. Neither supplies a matched endpoint metric for this family.

The resources a reader can reach are the project repositories, the Zenodo
archive, the hosted services, the Hub listings, Contrastive KERMT's model card,
weights and [code repository](https://github.com/NVIDIA-BioNeMo/KERMT), and the two ADMETlab papers
alongside the ADMET-AI paper. The distinction that governs all of them is the
one the record keeps making. A published benchmark split is not a training set
for the next model, and a catalogue of endpoints is not evidence that the
catalogue was filled from your chemical space.

## Worked case

The following is a **constructed teaching case**. It audits sentences against
records. It is not an executed session, a benchmark run, or a legal opinion.

A team wants to write one internal note about the open tooling available for
its analogue series. Nothing has been run. The note would contain six
sentences.

| Sentence the note would contain | Verdict | What the record says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ADMET-AI predicts ADME endpoints with Chemprop models trained on Therapeutics Data Commons datasets, and releases inference code, training code, and weights. | supported | All seven openness dimensions are established. The MIT file covers the package source, the bundled version 2 checkpoints, and the packaged CSV data files, and the archive on Zenodo holds the version 1 models, the data, the features, and the split files under a second licence that requires attribution. |
| ADMETlab 3.0 has the widest endpoint coverage in the family, at 34 ADME endpoints, 36 toxicity endpoints, and 8 toxicophore rules. | not supported | The site reports those three figures and separately reports that the predictable endpoint count rose from 88 to 119 while the underlying database spans 77 endpoints, and the page does not reconcile them. A coverage ranking between tools is also a comparison the records do not carry. |
| The ADMETlab model archives and the service output can be redistributed under one open licence. | not supported | The repository's terms are contested between the AGPL-3.0 LICENSE file and the New BSD licence named in the README. The 3.0 service states no licence, and its only LICENSE link returns HTTP 404. |
| Polaris can benchmark our model on a published split and place it beside the models listed on the Hub. | not supported | The client library can load splits and evaluate a method, but the Hub's curation and split rules are unpublished, and the models page lists six models with no weight or checkpoint download and no model-specific licence. |
| The Therapeutics Data Commons datasets behind our models are openly licensed and reproducible. | not supported | That record leaves its status, its access level, all seven openness dimensions, and every licence field unverified, and it records no licence at all. ADMET-AI's own record establishes the training data separately, because the archive ships it, and the curation, filtering, and split rules behind the released benchmark splits are not documented. |
| Contrastive KERMT directly predicts ADMET endpoints from an input molecule. | not supported | The record describes a pretrained backbone to fine-tune on the user's labelled endpoint data. It carries no prediction and no numerical benchmark. |

The first sentence is the only one the records carry. The second is the mistake
this lesson exists to prevent, because a wide endpoint count says nothing
about whether a prediction was made on a compound like yours. ADMETlab's
training database is not published, so the region of chemical space where its
predictions hold cannot be inspected, and the site does not reconcile its own
endpoint figures. The third and fourth sentences confuse permission with
capability. The fifth states a licence the record does not contain. The sixth
mistakes a representation backbone for an endpoint predictor.

## What this material does not show

Nothing here establishes that any of these tools predicts an endpoint
accurately for any compound, and nothing here compares them. The records carry
no matched protocol, no shared split, and no common metric, so a ranking of
the family would be an invention rather than a finding.

The records also do not establish that a prediction from one of these tools
transfers to a compound like yours. A published endpoint count is a catalogue
of what a service can be asked for. The domain of applicability lives in the
training data. ADMETlab's is unpublished, ADMET-AI's is released without its
curation and split rules, and the TDC record claims none. OpenADMET Models'
data are outside the scope of the licence that covers its code. Contrastive
KERMT's named pretraining pool was not inspected as files, contains no labelled
endpoint evaluation in this record, and cannot establish ADMET accuracy.

Contrastive KERMT states a representation and a pretraining objective. The
other records do not state a training objective, and their representation
detail does not go beyond the difference between the two ADMET-AI releases.
The KERMT card also cautions that predictions are statistical estimates and
must not substitute for experimental measurement in safety-critical decisions.

Currency is bounded as well. The older software records state the date of the
most recent commit they saw, and the two ADMETlab dates, 31 January 2024 for
the site and 20 June 2024 for the repository, are the ones that most limit what can be said
about that service. A released artefact is a dated statement about an interface
and a licence, not a standing property of a scientific field.

Finally, this reading is an inspection and not an execution. No model was run,
no weight was downloaded, no endpoint was predicted, and no benchmark score was
reproduced. A reader who wants a number from these tools has to run them under
a protocol they declare, and this page does not supply that protocol.

## Independent exercise

A project writes the following sentence for a report.

> We ranked our analogue series on ADMET risk using ADMETlab 3.0, which reports
> 34 ADME endpoints, 36 toxicity endpoints, and 8 toxicophore rules, and we
> checked the result against models listed on the Polaris Hub. We plan to
> publish the note and the service output under an open licence.

Answer the following using only the Atlas records for this family.

1. Separate the sentences in that note into a scientific claim, a capability
   claim, and a permission claim, and say which of the three the records
   support.
2. Say what the endpoint counts do establish and what they cannot establish
   about a compound like the one in the note.
3. Name the two licence questions the note has to answer before anything is
   published, and say which record answers each one.
4. Say what a matched comparison between the ADMETlab service and the Hub
   models would require, and why the records do not supply it.
5. Rewrite the note in one sentence that the records support.
