## What question does exposure answer?

Read [ADMET evaluation](/learn/admet-pk/admet-evaluation/) and
[assay controls and dose response](/learn/drug-discovery-foundations/assay-controls-dose-response/)
first. You will derive a concentration-time curve, separate clearance from a
decay constant, and explain why ranking compounds by one exposure number can fail.
All quantities below are invented for hand calculation. They are not patient
data, a dosing recommendation, or a validated candidate-selection rule.

An assay concentration describes what was present in a particular experimental
system. An administered amount does not directly specify the concentration at a
target over time. A PK model describes that time course. A PD model relates a
specified exposure to a specified response; neither model supplies missing
evidence that the response benefits the organism. The FDA's
[exposure-response guidance, sections IV–V](https://www.fda.gov/media/71277/download)
distinguishes exposure measures, response endpoints and their timing.

## Derive a minimal PK model

Let `A(t)` be amount in a single well-mixed compartment, `V` its apparent volume,
and `C(t) = A(t)/V` its concentration. Here `V` is an amount-to-concentration
ratio, not a literal anatomical container. Assume constant positive `V` and
clearance `CL`, first-order elimination, and no further input after an initial
amount `A₀` is placed directly in that compartment:

```text
dA/dt = −CL C = −(CL/V) A
k = CL/V
C(t) = (A₀/V) exp(−kt)
t½ = ln(2)/k
AUC₀→∞ = ∫₀∞ C(t) dt = A₀/CL
```

Separation gives `dA/A = −k dt`, hence exponential decay. Integrating that curve
gives the final expression. Clearance has volume/time units; `k` has inverse-time
units. They cannot be substituted for each other. The
[mrgsolve model specification](https://mrgsolve.org/user-guide/specification.html)
uses this amount/volume distinction and explicitly implements elimination as
`−(CL/V) × amount`.

For a total input amount `D` by another route, a fraction `F` reaching systemic
circulation gives `AUC₀→∞ = F D/CL` under linear, time-invariant elimination and
complete follow-up. This statement about integrated exposure does **not** make
oral absorption an instantaneous input. Unknown `F` means `D/AUC` identifies
apparent `CL/F`, not `CL`. Slow absorption or multiple compartments also prevents
reading the observed terminal slope as this one-compartment `CL/V` without checking
the model.

## Worked curve and what fitting would learn

Construct compound A with `A₀ = 40 µmol`, `V = 20 L`, and `CL = 2 L/h`.
Then `C(0) = 2 µmol/L`, `k = 0.1 h⁻¹`, half-life is about `6.931 h`, and AUC is
`20 µmol·h/L`. At one and two half-lives, concentrations are 1 and 0.5 µmol/L.
Those values come from the authored equation, not measurements.

If those three values had instead been measured without error, the log-concentration
slope would be `−0.1 h⁻¹`. A known initial systemic amount plus the intercept
would identify `V`; multiplying `k V` would identify `CL`. With noisy data, fit
parameters against observations, not against values generated by the same fitted
model. One explicit objective is `Σᵢ wᵢ[Cobs(tᵢ) − Cmodel(tᵢ; θ)]²`, where positive
weights specify the error model and `θ` contains the fitted parameters. Fitting
log concentrations instead changes how residuals are weighted.

A flexible curve that fits a few times is not evidence that its parameters are
identifiable. Retain route, timing, matrix, measurement limits and subject identity.
Repeated samples from one animal are a trajectory, not additional independent
animals. The [mrgsolve annotated model](https://mrgsolve.org/user-guide/topics.html#annotated-model-specification)
illustrates that structural equations, individual variation and observation
error are separate parts of a specification. No parameter-estimation software
is run in this lesson.

## Total exposure is not unbound exposure

Define `fᵤ` as the fraction unbound in the measured plasma matrix. For constant
binding, `Cᵤ(t) = fᵤ Ctotal(t)` and `AUCu = fᵤ AUCtotal`. Protein binding can vary
with concentration and context. Plasma unbound concentration is also not
automatically the target-site concentration. The FDA guidance's
[section V.C.1.e](https://www.fda.gov/media/71277/download) explains why total and
unbound measurements may support different PK/PD interpretations.

For a deliberately restricted direct-response example, assume target-site
concentration equals `Cᵤ`, response has no delay, and both compounds share
`EC50,u = 0.2 µmol/L`. Define the fractional effect increment:

```text
e(t) = Cᵤ(t) / [EC50,u + Cᵤ(t)]
```

This is a saturating Emax relationship normalized to a maximum increment of 1.
It is not a response probability or a binding constant. The
[Pharmpy direct-effect documentation](https://pharmpy.github.io/latest/api/pharmpy.modeling.set_direct_effect.html)
expresses a related Emax model with an explicit baseline and scale; setting its
baseline to zero would not reproduce our normalized increment. A delayed response
needs a different model, such as an effect compartment or turnover process, not
an unexplained horizontal shift of this curve.

Now give A `fᵤ = 0.05`. Construct B with the same initial amount and volume,
but `CL = 4 L/h` and `fᵤ = 0.20`:

| Quantity                               | Compound A | Compound B |
| -------------------------------------- | ---------- | ---------- |
| Total AUC, µmol·h/L                    | 20         | 10         |
| Unbound AUC, µmol·h/L                  | 1          | 2          |
| Initial unbound concentration, µmol/L  | 0.10       | 0.40       |
| Initial fractional effect increment    | 1/3        | 2/3        |
| Fractional effect increment at 6.931 h | 0.20       | 1/3        |

At that time, A's total concentration is 1; B has completed two half-lives and
its total concentration is 0.5 µmol/L. Multiplying by the respective fractions
gives unbound concentrations 0.05 and 0.10. Substitution gives the final row.
Thus A's larger total AUC does not give it the larger effect in this example.
The table compares complete assumed parameter sets: it does **not** predict
what happens if a chemist changes binding while somehow holding clearance fixed.
Effect increments at selected times are not integrated benefit or evidence that
B is safer. Neither construct has toxicity, selectivity or tissue-exposure data.

## Worked case: does one clearance assay predict another?

The previous sections use a single authored `CL`. Real compounds do not come
with one clearance number: the same compound measured in different assay
systems can disagree. The [AZ clearance-system
lab](/labs/az-clearance-system/) reads a real ChEMBL 37 dataset deposited
directly by AstraZeneca (document `CHEMBL3301361`, not a paywalled journal
article) reporting intrinsic clearance for real compounds in three named
systems: human liver microsomes, human hepatocytes, and rat hepatocytes.
Computed on 2026-09-12:

| Comparison | Overlap (compounds) | Spearman rho |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Human hepatocytes vs. rat hepatocytes (species differs, matrix fixed) | 106 | 0.4732 |
| Human liver microsomes vs. human hepatocytes (matrix differs, species fixed) | 96 | 0.4864 |
| Human liver microsomes vs. rat hepatocytes (both differ) | 359 | 0.3868 |

None of the three pairs is a strong predictor of another: every rho sits well
short of 1. The two hepatocyte assays share one unit, and named examples
there diverge by more than tenfold in either direction for the same real
compound; the microsome assay reports a different physical quantity (per
gram of protein, not per cell), so its divergence from either hepatocyte
assay is a real same-direction signal rather than a literal fold-change.
Either way, swapping species while holding the matrix fixed, or swapping
matrix while holding species fixed, changes the number enough that a single
"this compound's clearance" claim, without naming which of the three
systems produced it, cannot be checked against any one of these real
records.

**Independent exercise (naming the assay system):** A slide states "Compound
X has intrinsic clearance 18 mL/min/g." Using only the comparison above,
name two questions this sentence leaves unanswered, and say what each risks
if the answer changes.

<details data-worked-answer>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

The sentence does not say which species produced the number (human and rat
hepatocyte clearance for the same compound correlate at only rho ≈ 0.47 in
this real dataset, so a rat value is not a stand-in for a human one) and does
not say which matrix produced it (human microsome and human hepatocyte
clearance correlate at only rho ≈ 0.49, so a subcellular-fraction assay is
not a stand-in for a whole-cell one either). Getting either wrong risks
treating two real, differently-informative measurements as if they were the
same number, exactly the pooling this lesson's closing section warns
against.

</details>

## Independent exercise

Construct C from A by changing only the assumed volume to `40 L`, while retaining
`A₀ = 40 µmol`, `CL = 2 L/h`, `fᵤ = 0.05` and `EC50,u = 0.2 µmol/L`.
Calculate initial concentration, half-life, total AUC and initial effect increment.
Does a longer half-life imply more total exposure here? Separately, if an oral
experiment reports `D/AUC = 4 L/h` but does not establish `F`, can it establish
clearance of `4 L/h`?

<details data-worked-answer>
<summary>Worked answer — separate volume, clearance and response</summary>

C starts at `1 µmol/L`; `k = 0.05 h⁻¹`, so half-life is about `13.863 h`.
Total AUC remains `40/2 = 20 µmol·h/L`. Its initial unbound concentration is
`0.05 µmol/L`, giving an effect increment `0.05/(0.2 + 0.05) = 0.20`.
Increasing volume changes this curve's height and decay time, but not its area
when the initial amount and clearance are fixed.

The oral result identifies `CL/F = 4 L/h` under the stated linear assumptions.
For example, `F = 0.5` would imply `CL = 2 L/h`; `F = 1` would imply `4 L/h`.
The observation alone cannot choose between them. These calculations neither
select a human dose nor show that a target-site effect is beneficial.

</details>

## Return to the discovery decision

For an AIDD prediction, ask which measured quantity the label represents:
intrinsic clearance, systemic clearance, `CL/F`, total concentration, unbound
concentration, or a response. Pooling them as one “ADMET score” destroys the
interpretation just worked through, and the [AZ clearance-system
lab](/labs/az-clearance-system/) shows the same collapse on real data rather
than an authored example. Species, formulation, transport, active
metabolites and toxicity remain separate evidence gaps here. Continue to
[the AZ clearance-system lab](/labs/az-clearance-system/) to run the
species/matrix comparison yourself, or to [candidate
nomination](/learn/candidate-nomination/capstone/) with those gaps visible,
not converted into favorable predictions.
