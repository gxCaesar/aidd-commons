## Learning goals and prerequisites

You will read thirteen benchmark and evaluation cases, most of them published
in 2026, that examine whether reported numbers can be compared and what limits
their interpretation. You will see what each of them measures, what each of
them found, and what each of them explicitly does not license a reader to
conclude. You will do the
arithmetic behind one of them, because the arithmetic is the finding. Read
[Honest evaluation](/learn/evaluation-and-reproducibility/honest-evaluation/),
[Generalization unit and estimand](/learn/evaluation-and-reproducibility/generalization-unit-and-estimand/)
and [Leakage routes in an activity table](/learn/evaluation-and-reproducibility/split-leakage-routes/)
first. This is a reading and hand-arithmetic lesson: this project ran none of
these evaluations and measured none of their numbers.

## The cases, and the question each one asked

**PLABench** asked whether a reported deep-learning affinity number survives
the removal of the training corpora the benchmark audited. It runs nine
sequence- and structure-based models over the same targets under the same
metrics, and it separates three regimes: a warm start, a cold drug and a cold
target. On its ChEMBL35 arm the unfiltered set is 7,650 ligands across 84
targets. The benchmark's similarity filter drops every test ligand within an
ECFP4 Tanimoto of 0.85 of any ligand in the fourteen audited training
corpora, and then drops the targets left with fewer than ten ligands. The
resulting arm holds 4,862 ligands across 71 targets. The README reports that
one audited corpus, SAIR, reaches that similarity threshold against 32.7% of
the unfiltered benchmark, accounting for 2,413 removed rows, and that the
leading model still leads after the filter, with the README's own sentence
being that the filter is what makes the result readable.

**The Multi-Paradigm Docking Benchmark** asked whether a docking score and a
co-folding score could be placed on one leaderboard without misleading a
reader. It scores six physics-based docking approaches, Vina, GNINA, ICM,
ICM-RTCNN, SurfDock and UniDock2, and four co-folding models, AlphaFold 3,
Boltz-1, Chai-1 and Protenix, and it publishes the poses and the per-system
files alongside the aggregate tables.

**Beyond Affinity** asked what a generation method's number actually says when
the number is a docking score. It compares fifteen generation methods across
the one-dimensional, two-dimensional and three-dimensional paradigms, and it
reports that the three-dimensional methods generally produce stronger
affinities while their chemical validity and pose consistency may be weaker,
that the one-dimensional methods are dependable on the conventional molecular
metrics but seldom reach the best affinities, and that the two-dimensional
methods sit between them.

**BenchTox** asked whether the toxicity models in the literature could be
compared at all, given that the comparisons differed in datasets, in
preprocessing and in evaluation protocol. It evaluates more than twenty
approaches under consistent conditions across several datasets and several
partitioning strategies, adds a structured literature analysis, and releases
its framework as open-source software.

**ShallowBench** asked what happens when the targets are the hard ones. It
curates 5,780 shallow-pocket targets from CrossDocked2020, selected by
comparing the Alpha Shape lid volume with the protein's atom-voxel volume so
that the interfaces are shallow with low concavity and adequate binding
surface area, and it reports that the generative drug-design models it tested
produce weaker predicted binding affinity on them.

**Beyond Random Splits** asked whether the kind of distribution shift changes
which architecture looks best. It curates 718,800 unique drug-protein pairs
from ChEMBL and BindingDB, compares a Morgan-fingerprint and protein-CNN
baseline with twelve further architectures built from four drug
representations and three ESM-2 interaction modes, and reports that the
architectures agree at a rank correlation of 0.79 under two chemical shifts,
0.39 under a protein shift, and -0.55 under a shift that combines them,
where the order reverses.

**CryptoBench** asked what it takes to call a binding site cryptic. It defines
one by an apo and holo structure pair, publishes the creation scripts under an
MIT licence, and puts the dataset on a public Open Science Framework project
that states no licence.

**PLINDER** asked what a protein-ligand interaction dataset has to do before
its numbers can be compared with anyone else's. It versions the dataset by the
month of the release, so a number is always about a named version, and it
publishes pre-set train, validation and test splits curated to minimise
information leakage, with test subsets of novel proteins, pockets,
interactions and ligands.

**CACHE Challenge** asked what a benchmark looks like when the number is
produced by an experiment that happens after the prediction. In its
retrospective phase participants retrieve hits from an affinity selection mass
spectrometry screen of 400,000 compounds; the top ten are then invited to
predict novel hits from the Enamine catalogue, which the organisers procure
and test experimentally at their own cost, and the winners are decided on the
number of novel chemical series found in that prospective phase.

**PoseBench** asked how docking and co-folding methods behave with predicted
protein structures, unknown binding pockets or multiple ligands. Its toolkit
provides shared inference, relaxation, alignment and scoring workflows,
datasets and benchmark predictions. Its paper reports method comparisons.

**ProtDBench** asked what happens when the verifier is part of the
measurement. It fixes the tasks, the protocol and the success criteria,
evaluates structure-prediction models used as verifiers against wet-lab
annotated data, and compares representative open-source generative methods on
ten protein targets.

**MAGI** is an agent study asking what limits a real lead-optimisation project.
It replays nine historical projects from three pharmaceutical companies under
fixed temporal cutoffs, with an LLM generation route and REINVENT 4 behind
interchangeable scoring services.

**BioStudyBench** asked how data analysis changes an agent's agreement with
published findings beyond its prior knowledge. Its 25 tasks come from studies
published after the evaluated models' developer-reported knowledge cutoffs.
Each task runs with and without data and tool access, with three repeats per
condition.

## What each one licenses, and what each one does not

Across these domains, the studies make parts of the measurement process
explicit and show how they affect the reported result.

PLABench's finding is not that the leading model changes. It is that the
number changes when the leak is removed, and that the direction of the change
depends on the regime: the README gives MixingDTA's RMSE rising from 0.462 to
0.541 on Davis and from 0.404 to 0.598 on KIBA as the evaluation moves from a
warm start to a cold target. A reader who reports one RMSE without naming the
regime has not reported the measurement. What the benchmark does not do is
settle which model is best, because the answer it gives is that the ranking
survives the filter while the absolute numbers do not.

The Multi-Paradigm Docking Benchmark's finding is about the granularity of the
report rather than about the systems. It publishes per-system ranking scores
alongside the aggregate tables, and its own stated point is that the aggregate
is misleading when a co-folding model has memorised training-like targets, so
a result has to be read inside a similarity stratum. What it does not do is
provide a single ordering, and a reader who takes the aggregate tables alone
loses the benchmark's main finding.

Beyond Affinity's finding is that affinity is not a complete success
criterion, because a favourable binding score can coexist with invalid
chemistry or with an inaccurate docking pose. The same benchmark's affinity is
a docking oracle score rather than an experimental measurement, so its
ordering inherits the known biases of docking scores. What it does not do is
establish a best paradigm, because it reports a trade-off and not a winner.

BenchTox's finding is that consistent conditions are worth more than another
method, because the literature's comparisons were hard to interpret for
reasons of protocol rather than of chemistry. What it does not do is compare
graph-based approaches with descriptor or fingerprint baselines: its scope is
graph-based models, so a reader who takes its ordering as a field-wide
ranking has read more into it than the paper claims.

ShallowBench's finding is about the target family rather than the
architecture, and its affinity is a predicted one, so the ordering inherits the
biases of the scoring function that produced it. What it does not do is
establish that a method fails on shallow pockets in general: the targets come
from one geometric selection rule, and a reader who reads the number as a
statement about all difficult targets is generalising past the selection.

Beyond Random Splits' finding is that the error and the ranking move
differently, which is the reason the paper's own conclusion is about the
extrapolation setting rather than about average error. The validation RMSE
rises from 0.950 and 0.945 under the two chemical shifts to 1.299 and 1.321
under the protein and dual shifts, a modest change, while the agreement
between architectures falls from 0.79 to 0.39 and then reverses to -0.55. A
reader who chose an architecture on a chemical shift and then read the
biological shift's error would not notice that the choice had become the
opposite one. What the study does not do is tell the reader which shift they
will face.

CryptoBench's finding is that the definition decides the dataset: a ligand
that binds a pocket absent in the apo structure is cryptic by construction,
and a pocket present but differently shaped is not. What it does not do is
license the use of the data. The scripts are MIT and the dataset is public,
and the project states no licence, so a reader who needs a grant for the
files has to ask the authors.

PLINDER's finding is that the split construction is the contribution and the
version is the other half of it. The test subsets are chosen for novel
proteins, pockets, interactions and ligands, so a number reported against a
random split of the same molecules is not the same measurement, and a number
reported against last month's release is not the same measurement either.
What it does not do is settle the terms of the data: the repository's
Apache-2.0 grant covers the code and the generator, the dataset is served
from a public monthly directory, and the project site states no terms for it.

CACHE's finding is that the judging number is produced by an experiment that
happens after the prediction, which is the design that separates it from a
retrospective benchmark. What it does not do is make the retrospective phase
comparable with anything else: those hits are retrieved from a screen the
organisers had already run, and the score that decides the round comes from
the prospective phase alone.

PoseBench also exposes a measurement correction. Its repository notice records
a scoring bug in version 1.0.0 affecting the primary-ligand datasets Astex
Diverse, DockGen-E and PoseBusters Benchmark. Corrected version 1.1.0 reports
about 15 percent lower performance on average for each benchmarked method and
advises rerunning affected analyses. This is neither a 15-percentage-point
reduction nor a universal adjustment across datasets. Its method comparisons
remain tied to their dataset, metric and version.

ProtDBench finds verifier-dependent bias and limited agreement under identical
filtering protocols. Its filtering rules, success definitions and
throughput-aware accounting change conclusions about efficiency, success rate
and diversity. It releases an Apache-2.0 toolkit and reports method
comparisons. Success for its newly generated binders means passing a
computational filter, not a new experimental hit. Biases measured on the Cao
energy-designed candidates may not fully transfer to deep generative designs.

MAGI finds that objective attainment tracked scorer accuracy and applicability
rather than the generation route. Four blinded chemists could not reliably
distinguish agent proposals from held-out compounds and judged the SAR
reasoning plausible but incomplete. Four campaigns use published GSK series,
four use Sanofi RetroDMTA data, and one AstraZeneca programme remains
confidential. Proposal endpoints are predictions, not new measurements. One
backend and trajectory per condition, with no static-objective control, leave
the agent's causal contribution to attainment unresolved.

BioStudyBench reports that data and tools raise average pass rate by 47
percentage points across eight models. The strongest open-weight model scores
81.3 percent, compared with 94.7 percent for the strongest closed-weight
model. This comparison does not identify a causal effect of weight access.
Passing measures reference agreement within the scoring tolerance, not
comprehensive methodological correctness. Temporal cutoffs and restricted
retrieval reduce contamination risk without proving every answer was derived
independently.

That is the transferable lesson, and it is the same one this project applies
to its own material. A benchmark's headline number is a statement about a
protocol. The part of it that names the protocol is not a weakness of the
number; it is the part that makes the number usable.

## Handworked exercise

**Handworked exercise (not runnable or empirical):** A benchmark's unfiltered
arm holds 7,650 ligands across 84 targets. Its similarity filter removes
2,413 rows, and a second rule then removes every target left with fewer than
ten ligands, leaving an arm of 4,862 ligands across 71 targets. A second
benchmark compares fifteen generation methods across three paradigms and
reports that the three-dimensional methods reach the stronger affinities while
their validity and pose consistency may be weaker. A third benchmark evaluates
more than twenty approaches, all of them graph-based, under consistent
conditions. (a) How many ligands does the similarity filter leave, and what
fraction of the unfiltered arm is that? (b) How many rows does the sparse-target
rule then remove, and which of the two rules does the larger share of the
work? (c) State one number you would not report from the second benchmark
without naming the regime, and one conclusion you would not draw from the
third benchmark's ordering. (d) For each of the three, name the measurement
that would turn its finding into a general claim about the field.

<details data-worked-answer>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

(a) 7,650 − 2,413 = 5,237 ligands, which is 68.5% of the unfiltered arm.
(b) The sparse-target rule then removes 5,237 − 4,862 = 375 rows, so the
similarity filter removed about six and a half times as many rows as the
target rule did. The two rules are not equivalent, and a reader who reports
"we removed the near-duplicates" has named only the rule that did most of the
work. (c) From the second benchmark, the affinity figure alone: it is a
docking-oracle score, so it inherits the docking function's biases, and it
says nothing about the chemical validity that the same benchmark reports
separately. From the third benchmark, any statement of the form "graph-based
models outperform simpler methods", because the comparison it ran was among
graph-based models and a descriptor or fingerprint baseline was not among
them. (d) For the first, a second target set built under a different corpus,
so that the leakage-control result would not be a property of the fourteen
audited corpora. For the second, experimental affinities rather than docking
oracle scores, so that the trade-off between affinity and validity would be
measured against the same quantity a reader would act on. For the third, the
same protocol run over descriptor and fingerprint baselines, so that the
ordering would say something about paradigms rather than about the graph-based
family alone.
