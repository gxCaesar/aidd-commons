## Learning goals and prerequisites

You will read two route-planning systems released in 2026 and notice that both
move the work from proposing a route to searching a memory under a budget.
You will read one system's published reward function, which is a set of
constants rather than a description, and work out what those constants
reward and what they leave unrewarded. You will read the other system's
published metric definition and its sampling caveat, and see what its headline
number is a statement about. Read [Reaction mapping and route
search](/learn/synthesis-and-automation/reaction-mapping-and-route-search/)
and [Route feasibility, forward prediction, and
feedback](/learn/synthesis-and-automation/route-feasibility-and-feedback/)
first. This is a reading and hand-arithmetic lesson: this project ran neither
system, synthesised no compound and executed no route.

## The mechanism: a route as a search with a budget

Both systems treat route planning as search over an explicit memory rather
than as one forward pass over a model. In RetroAgent, the memory is a
structured AND-OR graph held by a tool server; the agent issues tool calls
that expand the graph, and the route it returns is a path through what the
search actually built. In Agentic-Syn-RRAG, the workflow is named
Generate-Retrieve-Refine: a locally served fine-tuned model proposes
candidate reactions by reaction-state beam search, RXNGraphormer embeddings
drive a FAISS search that retrieves ten structurally similar patent
precedents, a reranking model selects the three most relevant of them, and a
main model then refines the proposal against that retrieved evidence into a
structured protocol specifying reactants, reagents, solvents, quantities,
temperature and reaction time.

A budget makes the search a decision rather than a procedure, and it is the
budget that the training and the evaluation both turn on. RetroAgent's
released reward is worth reading as code rather than as prose, because its
constants are the whole specification. A rollout that completes a route earns
a base of +1, reduced by a budget penalty of 0.02 per single-step model call
beyond twenty calls, capped at 0.3; and by a depth penalty of 0.02 per step
beyond route depth twenty, capped at 0.3; and by 0.05 for each turn with a
failed tool call and 0.05 for each turn with no parseable tool call. The
total is clipped to the interval from -1 to 1. A rollout that does not
complete a route earns -1.0, or -1.5 if it produced no valid tool call at
all.

Two consequences follow from those constants and are worth carrying. First,
the budget penalties bind only on a completed route, so an expensive failure
and a cheap failure receive the same flat -1.0. The reward is shaped around
search-budget efficiency for routes that exist; it does not, as released,
distinguish a trajectory that failed after five calls from one that failed
after two hundred. Second, the thresholds are part of the released
configuration and not implementation detail: a reader who changes twenty, or
the 0.02 rate, or either cap, is no longer running the released agent, and
the published behaviour no longer describes what the reader has in hand.

## Worked case: two systems, two kinds of published evidence

RetroAgent is described in a README and a BibTeX entry for a Conference on
Language Modeling 2026 paper by Zhu, Gan, Sun, Sun, Shi, Islam, Shang, Gao,
Coley, Sun and Wang. The README specifies the method and the training
recipe in unusual detail: the policy is trained end to end with
reinforcement learning using GSPO, a sequence-level clipping method, on the
Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 base model, inside the slime framework with Megatron
and SGLang, on a single eight-GPU H200 node in colocated mode, with the
training and inference dependencies supplied by a pinned Docker image. What
the README does not carry is a route-quality measurement, and this lesson
therefore quotes no route accuracy for it. Its openness is also layered, and
each layer has its own terms: the repository code is MIT, the released
weights on Hugging Face are Apache-2.0, the weights repository labels the
DESP-sourced data CC BY 4.0 and the SCScore data MIT, and the NOTICE file
states that the single-step retrosynthesis model vendored under
`external/desp/` is derived from the DESP work of Yu and colleagues
(NeurIPS 2024) and remains subject to that project's own licence and
attribution. So the MIT grant on the repository does not reach the vendored
component, and "open weights" here is not the same as "the whole system is
MIT".

Agentic-Syn-RRAG, by Shan Zhu, Xin Chen, Pinqi Wang and seven further
authors, is a ChemRxiv preprint, version 1 of 2026 under DOI
10.26434/chemrxiv.15008409/v1, with its code in the repository
SamSamChu/Agentic-Syn-RRAG under the MIT licence. Its README reports its own
numbers, and they are the more interesting case because the evaluation
protocol is stated alongside them. The evaluation set is 150 targets
randomly sampled from the product-disjoint WIPO-2M held-out test set. On
those 150 targets at Top-1, the README reports reactant accuracy of 48.0%
for the full Syn-RRAG pipeline, 46.0% for the SFT Llama baseline and 14.7%
for native Gemini-2.5-Pro, and rule checks giving validity of 100.0%,
elemental consistency of 100.0% and structural compatibility of 99.3%. It
separately reports the standalone SFT backbone on the whole WIPO-2M held-out
test of about ten thousand products at Top-1/3/5/10 of 44.5%, 65.1%, 70.8%
and 75.9%.

Two features of that protocol change how the numbers should be read. The
metric is pooled: reactant accuracy requires every reference reactant
component to appear in the pooled predicted reactants and reagents after
RDKit canonicalization, and the two fields are pooled because a model may
classify a true reactant as a reagent. And the Top-k numbers are prefixes:
the README states that Top-1, Top-3 and Top-5 taken from a completed Top-10
run are prefixes of the final pooled ranking and need not equal independent
runs made with a smaller sample count. A reader who compares a prefix number
against an independent-run number is comparing two different procedures, and
the README says so before anyone asks.

The arithmetic is worth doing by hand because the percentages hide it. On
150 targets, 48.0% is 72 targets, 46.0% is 69, and 14.7% is 22 (22 out of
150 is 14.67%). So the full system beats its own SFT baseline by three
targets out of one hundred and fifty, and one target is worth 0.67
percentage points, while the native model is fifty targets behind. The
README reports no confidence interval and no paired test for that
three-target gap, and a gap of three targets on a single sampled set is not
by itself a separation. The two systems are also not comparable in what they
optimise: RetroAgent searches a graph memory under a call budget, and
Syn-RRAG grounds a protocol in retrieved patent precedents, and a protocol
that cites a precedent is not the same claim as a route found with fewer
expansions.

Finally, the openness of the second system has a limit that the MIT licence
does not show. The code is open, but its default configuration drives
reranking and protocol refinement with an external model over an
OpenAI-compatible endpoint, with a default reranking model of
gemini-2.5-flash and separate credentials for the main model and the
reranking model. A reader reproducing the system needs those endpoints and
their terms, so an open repository is not a self-contained system here.
Reproducing the training is a further step again: the README applies a patch
to LLaMA-Factory at a pinned commit, because the patch modifies internal
data loading, SFT workflow and trainer files, and it recommends Python 3.12
with CUDA 12.8 and four GPUs.

## Alternatives, limits, and independent exercise

What this lesson does not establish is specific. It does not establish that
either system produces a route that is executable or correct; a route is a
proposal, and the protocol a second system returns is a proposal with
citations attached. It does not establish that the three targets the second
system's README describes as wet-lab confirmed are representative, since the
README itself describes them as a separate short demonstration rather than
part of the 150-target evaluation set. It does not establish a comparison
between the two systems, because they were not compared by anyone here and
they optimise different things. And it does not establish the route-quality
figure that RetroAgent's paper presumably reports, because the paper was not
opened for this lesson.

**Handworked exercise (not runnable or empirical):** A route-planning agent's
released reward gives +1 for a completed route, minus a budget penalty of
0.02 per single-step model call beyond twenty calls capped at 0.3, minus a
depth penalty of 0.02 per step beyond depth twenty capped at 0.3, minus 0.05
per turn with a failed tool call and 0.05 per turn with no parseable tool
call, clipped to the interval from -1 to 1, with -1.0 for an incomplete route
and -1.5 when no valid tool call was made at all. Separately, a
retrieval-augmented planner reports 48.0% reactant accuracy at Top-1 on 150
targets, 46.0% for its own fine-tuned baseline on the same 150 targets, and
14.7% for a native general-purpose model, with its metric pooling predicted
reactants and reagents, and with its Top-k numbers taken as prefixes of a
completed Top-10 ranking. (a) Compute the reward for a completed route with
45 single-step model calls, deepest route depth 35, and two failed tool
calls. (b) Compute the reward for a completed route with 20 calls, depth 20
and no failed tool calls, and state which term you expected to bind. (c)
Compute the reward for an incomplete route with 200 single-step model calls
and depth 60, and state what that says about what the released reward
penalises. (d) Convert the three accuracies into counts out of 150, compute
the gap between the full system and its own baseline in targets, and state
what would have to be reported before that gap could be read as a
separation. (e) Name one thing the pooling rule makes easier to score than
it would be without pooling, and say what the pooling rule costs.

<details data-worked-answer>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

(a) The budget penalty is 0.02 × (45 − 20) = 0.5, capped at 0.3. The depth
penalty is 0.02 × (35 − 20) = 0.3, at its cap. The tool penalty is 2 × 0.05
= 0.10. The reward is clip(−1, 1, 1.0 − 0.3 − 0.3 − 0.10) = 0.30. (b) Both
penalties are zero because twenty calls and depth twenty are exactly the
thresholds, and there are no failed tool calls, so the reward is clip(−1, 1,
1.0) = 1.0. The term you might expect to bind is the one that binds earliest,
the call penalty, and it does not bind at all at twenty calls. (c) An
incomplete route earns −1.0 regardless of how much budget it consumed, so
200 calls and depth 60 change nothing: the reward is −1.0. This is the
sharpest point about the released constants: the budget penalties are applied
to completed routes, so an expensive failure and a cheap failure are
indistinguishable in the reward, and the "search-budget efficiency" framing
describes the behaviour on routes that succeed. (d) 48.0% of 150 is 72
targets, 46.0% is 69, and 14.7% is 22. The full system beats its own
baseline by three targets out of 150, one target being 0.67 percentage
points, and trails the native model by fifty. Before that three-target gap
could be read as a separation, the report would have to give an interval or
a paired test over the same sampled targets, and would have to fix the
comparison at one sample count rather than comparing a prefix of a Top-10
ranking against something else. (e) Pooling lets a model that puts a true
reactant in its reagents still be scored correct, which is the chemically
fairer treatment and inflates the score relative to a strict reactant-only
rule; it costs the ability to distinguish a model that identifies the right
molecule from one that misassigns its role, and it makes the number depend
on a field convention the reader cannot see in the percentage.

</details>
