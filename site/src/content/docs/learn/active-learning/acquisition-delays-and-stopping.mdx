## Select an experiment, not just a prediction

Read [experiment pools and budgets](/learn/active-learning/experiment-pools-and-budgets/),
[diversity, uncertainty and feedback](/learn/active-learning/diversity-uncertainty-feedback/)
and [uncertainty and decisions](/learn/qsar-properties/uncertainty-to-decisions/).
This lesson derives a simple acquisition score and follows a delayed-feedback
timeline. Its numbers are constructed; no new campaign is run here.

Pure exploitation selects the candidate with the best predicted outcome. An
experiment-selection policy may instead pay for information that improves later
choices. The objective might be the best feasible value found, number of confirmed
hits, integrated utility over a budget, or information about a mechanism. These
are different objectives. The acquisition function must match the declared one.

Uncertainty sampling favors uncertain predictions. It can spend the entire
budget on irreducibly noisy or unmeasurable objects. Diversity sampling covers
different regions but may ignore the target endpoint. A useful comparison includes
competent fixed policies under the same initial observations, eligible pool,
measurement cost and stopping rule. The [existing pool-budget lab](/labs/pool-budget-campaign/)
provides a scoped reference; its retrospective measured pool is not a prospective
wet-lab campaign.

## Expected improvement by hand

Assume a **maximization** problem, a Gaussian predictive distribution
`Y(x) ~ Normal(μ(x), σ²(x))`, and a known current best value `b`. Improvement
is `I(x)=max(Y(x)−b,0)`. For `σ>0`, define `z=(μ−b)/σ`; then

```text
EI(x) = (μ−b) Φ(z) + σ φ(z)
```

Here `Φ` and `φ` are the standard normal cumulative distribution and density.
At zero predictive standard deviation, EI reduces to `max(μ−b,0)`.
The [BoTorch acquisition reference](https://botorch.readthedocs.io/en/stable/acquisition.html)
documents analytic and Monte Carlo acquisition functions, including their
assumptions. Noisy observations and batch selection require treatment beyond
pretending the largest noisy observation is a known latent optimum.

Construct `b=5` and candidate A with `μ=5`, `σ=1`. Because `z=0`, its first
term is zero and `EI(A)=φ(0)=1/√(2π)≈0.3989`. Candidate B has the same mean
but `σ=2`, giving EI about 0.7979. Candidate C is deterministic at 5.3,
giving EI 0.3. Under this particular score, B ranks above A above C. That does
not prove B has the largest immediate success probability or is experimentally
feasible. Its favorable score depends on a meaningful predictive uncertainty.

For a minimization endpoint, reverse the improvement direction; do not reuse the
maximization formula with an unchanged incumbent. Upper confidence bound is a
different score, commonly `μ+κσ` for maximization with dimensionless `κ`.
If an implementation writes `sqrt(β)σ`, its exploration coefficient is
`κ=sqrt(β)`, not `β`. Confusing variance with standard deviation also changes
the units and policy. The exploration setting is part of the declared recipe.

## Cost, constraints and the batch are part of the objective

Under a simple immediate gain-per-cost heuristic, A costing 1 unit has
`EI/cost≈0.3989`, while B costing 4 units has about 0.1995. This ordering favors
A, reversing raw EI. It is a heuristic, not a general solution to a finite-horizon
allocation problem. Very cheap redundant candidates can consume a batch without
adding useful information. Similarly, the sum of individual EIs need not equal
the expected improvement of a jointly selected, correlated batch.

Feasibility can be a hard eligibility constraint or an explicitly modelled uncertain
event. A candidate that cannot be synthesized or measured in the available system
is not an experiment simply because the predictor accepts its feature vector.
Multiplying improvement by a feasibility probability requires assumptions about
their relationship; independence cannot be silently assumed when the same molecular
property drives both. Keep failed synthesis, failed measurement and valid low
activity as different outcomes.

## Delayed feedback changes what the policy knows

Construct a campaign with these events:

| Time | Event                                          | Labels available for the next fitted predictor |
| ---- | ---------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| t0   | Initial observations completed; select A and B | Initial set only                               |
| t1   | A returns a valid result; B remains pending    | Initial set plus A                             |
| t2   | Select C; B still pending                      | Initial set plus A                             |
| t3   | B returns a valid result                       | Initial set plus A and B                       |
| t4   | C fails measurement quality requirements       | Same valid labels; separate C failure record   |

At t2, B is not a zero, a negative or an observed label. Its pending status can
inform a batch-aware policy, but its eventual value is unavailable. Replaying
the campaign with B's t3 label at t2 gives the policy future information.
Record selection time, result-availability time and outcome status separately.
A selected candidate may consume cost before its label arrives; wall-clock
progress and completed-label count are therefore different budget axes.

At t4, decide whether a retry is eligible under the already stated engineering
and assay rules. Do not convert a failed measurement into low biological activity.
If success or delay depends on chemistry, the completed-label subset is selected;
a policy evaluated only on completed successes can look better by avoiding its
own difficult outcomes. Include attempts and failures in the cost account.

## Stop and evaluate the whole policy

Declare a fixed attempt budget, cost ceiling, time horizon or externally defined
success criterion before comparing policies. A stopping rule based on recent
improvement is itself a policy component. Applying it only to a struggling
baseline makes the comparison asymmetric. An apparently flat learning curve
does not by itself prove that no useful candidate remains.

For retrospective replay, reveal only eligible labels at their specified times.
Use common starting pools and paired campaign instances where justified; summarize
the difference at a fixed budget or a prespecified area under the campaign curve.
Multiple random seeds on one fixed measured pool describe that algorithm's
variation on that pool. They do not create independent prospective campaigns
or establish transfer to a different assay.

Keep final evaluation separate from acquisition tuning. Choosing exploration
strength, stopping patience or cost weights after comparing final replay curves
is development. A confirmatory policy comparison needs an untouched campaign
set or a clearly separate prospective evaluation. Preserve a fixed policy's win
when observed; uncertainty-based selection is a hypothesis, not a guaranteed upgrade.

## Independent exercises

1. With `μ=b` and `σ=0.5`, calculate EI. What does EI become for `σ=0` and
   `μ=b−1`?
2. With `μ=5`, `σ=2` and `κ=1.5`, calculate the upper confidence bound. What
   value of `β` gives the same `sqrt(β)σ` coefficient?
3. At t2, a replay table already contains B's final result. May the model use it?
   How should C's t4 failure enter the label and cost tables?
4. Policy P makes 10 attempts and gets 8 valid labels; Q makes 8 attempts and
   gets 8 valid labels at equal attempt cost. Are their budgets matched?

<details data-worked-answer>
<summary>Worked answers — use the information and budget actually available</summary>

EI is `0.5/√(2π)≈0.1995`. At zero variance and a mean below the incumbent it is
zero. UCB is `5+1.5×2=8`, with `β=2.25`. B's final value is unavailable at t2
despite existing in the retrospective file. C contributes a failed-attempt status
and incurred cost, not an invented activity label. P and Q have equal valid-label
counts but different attempt budgets, 10 versus 8. Match the declared budget axis
or explicitly analyse the different cost and completion outcomes.
