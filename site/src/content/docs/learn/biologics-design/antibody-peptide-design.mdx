## Learning goals and prerequisites

You will define an antibody or peptide design object, explain why affinity and
specificity are distinct, and write positive and negative selection conditions
that test a named off-target alternative. Read [Biologics and multimodal
design](/learn/biologics-design/multimodal-design/) first. This is a conceptual,
handworked lesson: it contains no selection recipe, expression instruction, or
therapeutic dosing advice.

## Design begins with the recognition event

For an antibody, the object may be a variable-region sequence, format, and a
specified antigen state. For a peptide, it may be a sequence and constrained or
unconstrained state. The desired output must name the antigen form, epitope or
functional mechanism when known, desired context, and negative set. “Binds
target” is underspecified when the target has homologues, multiple conformations,
post-translational states, or shared surfaces.

Affinity is a concentration-dependent interaction quantity under named assay
conditions. Specificity is comparative: a candidate's interaction with intended
object(s) versus a declared universe of plausible alternatives. A high apparent
affinity can coexist with broad non-specific interaction; a selective candidate
may have inadequate functional activity. Peptide-directed antibody design itself
requires trade-offs among uniqueness, conservation, accessible features, and
modification-sensitive regions [Pisitkun et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21956165/).

For a simple equilibrium illustration, `K_D = [P][L]/[PL]`, where `P` is free
protein, `L` free ligand, and `PL` complex at equilibrium. Its unit is mol/L;
lower `K_D` means more complex at a given matched total concentration. This is
not a universal result for avid, multivalent, kinetic, membrane, or cell-based
systems. An apparent `K_D` must retain format, valency, temperature, buffer, and
analysis model. It cannot alone establish epitope, selectivity in biology, or
function.

## Positive and negative selection make the target claim testable

A positive condition retains candidates that recognize the intended antigen
state. A negative condition removes or flags candidates that recognize a named
competitor: a close homologue, irrelevant protein with similar presentation, a
different antigen state, matrix component, or a cell population lacking the
intended marker. The negative set is a scientific choice. “No binding to one
unrelated control” does not demonstrate broad specificity, whereas screening an
unrealistically vast negative universe can erase a useful context-specific
binder. State what the negative panel represents and what it misses.

Use a contrast rather than a generic activity score. Let `R+` and `R−` be
background-corrected signals for matched positive and negative conditions. A
constructed selectivity contrast is `ΔR = R+ − R−`; larger positive values are
better only if high signal has the same meaning in both conditions. It must not
be compared across assay formats as if it were `K_D`. More importantly, a signal
difference can come from antigen amount, display geometry, detector response, or
candidate aggregation; matched controls and orthogonal confirmation address
different alternatives.

## Worked case: a constructed negative-selection decision

All values here are **constructed for teaching**. Two antibody candidates are
measured in one matched detection format. The intended antigen state gives
signals `R+ = 92` for A and `88` for B. A close homologue gives `R− = 38` for A
and `12` for B. Thus `ΔR_A = 92 − 38 = 54` and `ΔR_B = 88 − 12 = 76` arbitrary
signal units. A generic-protein control gives 8 for A and 10 for B.

If the prespecified gate is `R+ ≥ 70` and `R− ≤ 20` for the close homologue,
A fails and B passes this **panel**. B is not “specific” in an absolute sense:
the panel omitted other family members, unrelated sticky interactions, and the
cellular context. The next informative question is whether B's signal persists
when the antigen is presented in a second relevant format, and whether its
functional response is absent in a target-negative matched context. Those tests
probe presentation and functional causality rather than merely adding repeats.

For a peptide, the same logic applies, but conformational and proteolytic state
can change the object itself. A sequence predicted to form a desired helix is a
representation-level proposal; a functional comparison must specify whether the
peptide actually adopts that state in the evaluation system. Do not call a
sequence model “binding validation.”

## Alternatives, limitations, and independent exercise

Deep mutational scanning, structural mapping, cell panels, and polyreactivity
panels can refine a negative set, but each has coverage and representation
limits. Computational sequence scores can prioritize variants, not replace the
positive/negative evidence contract. Biophysical developability is also not
specificity: an antibody may avoid aggregation yet bind an unintended target, or
vice versa. Observed self-interaction and polyspecificity differences across
discovery sources illustrate why selection history should be recorded, not used
as a deterministic label [Kaleli et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30883916/).

**Handworked exercise (not runnable or empirical):** Under the same constructed
rules, peptide P has `R+ = 74`, `R− = 19`; peptide Q has `R+ = 81`, `R− = 27`.
Calculate both `ΔR` values, decide which passes the panel, and name one
alternative explanation remaining for P. Why is “P has lower K_D” not justified
from this table?

<details data-worked-answer>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

`ΔR_P = 74 − 19 = 55`; `ΔR_Q = 81 − 27 = 54` arbitrary units. P meets both
constructed thresholds, while Q fails the negative signal threshold despite a
high positive signal. P could still reflect antigen-presentation or detector
effects, a missing homologue, or aggregation. The table contains signals rather
than an equilibrium analysis with free concentrations, so it cannot establish a
lower `K_D` for P.

</details>
