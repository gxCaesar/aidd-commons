## Learning goals and prerequisites

After this reading-and-handworked lesson, you can write a target product profile
(TPP) as a decision contract, distinguish a threshold from a preference, and use
an analogue series to test a structure–property hypothesis. Read [Drug discovery
context](/learn/drug-discovery-foundations/discovery-context/) and [Assay controls
and dose response](/learn/drug-discovery-foundations/assay-controls-dose-response/)
first. This is not a synthesis, assay, clinical-dosing, or patient-selection
protocol.

## A TPP is a falsifiable product decision, not a wish list

A TPP starts with the person or biological context in which a later decision
would be made. It then states the intended effect, route or format at a high
level, evidence needed to act, and **minimum** and **preferred** attributes. A
minimum is a gate: below it, the candidate does not advance on this rationale. A
preferred value is a direction for trade-off discussions, not a claim that all
attributes can be simultaneously optimized. Accessible TPPs vary widely in the
number of features and often omit their development method; that is a reason to
record assumptions, not to fill every row with a number [Ibnidris et
al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39075460/).

The object in a TPP is a candidate **for a stated decision**, not a molecule in
isolation. The representation may be a chemical structure, a series identifier,
and a table of endpoint records. The objective is a constrained selection rule,
not a scalar score: retain candidates that meet all prespecified gates, then
describe trade-offs among survivors. A later observed value may update the TPP
only with a dated rationale; it must not silently redefine the gate.

## A chemical series is an intervention on a hypothesis

An analogue series changes one interpretable feature while holding as much else
as practical constant. For a pair, define a signed response difference
`Δy = y(analogue B) − y(analogue A)` under the same endpoint, unit, assay
context, and aggregation rule. If `y` is `pIC50 = −log10(IC50 in mol/L)`, then a
positive `Δy` means lower `IC50`, hence greater apparent potency under that
assay's conditions. It does **not** establish affinity, mechanism, selectivity,
or transport. A matched molecular pair can support a narrow claim such as “this
change coincided with a 0.30-unit increase in this endpoint,” not “this group
caused efficacy.”

Feedback has three roles. First, it checks whether the proposed causal feature
predicts direction across more than one comparison. Second, it exposes conflicts:
an apparent potency gain may accompany worse solubility, stability, or a changed
assay control window. Third, it picks the next _informative_ comparison, often a
cheap control that separates two explanations. Do not average endpoints with
unknown units merely to obtain a series score.

## Worked case: a constructed advance decision

Everything in this case is **constructed for teaching**. A team has a
target-proximal cellular endpoint and declares the following TPP fragment before
seeing its next analogues: normalized inhibition at 1 µM must be at least 60%; a
solubility proxy must be at least 20 arbitrary units; a reporter counterscreen
must show less than 15% inhibition. The preferred cellular `pIC50` is at least
6.30. These are invented gates, not a recommendation for any programme.

For A→B, `ΔpIC50 = 6.45 − 6.10 = +0.35`. Because `pIC50` is logarithmic, the
constructed concentration ratio is `10^0.35 = 2.24`: B's fitted `IC50` would be
about 2.24-fold lower if both fits use the same definition. That numerical gain
does not override B's failed gate. C has `ΔpIC50 = +0.22` and passes all three
invented gates, so the next decision is not “C succeeds.” It is “test whether C's
cellular signal persists in an orthogonal target-proximal readout and whether the
series relation remains when an independently prepared batch is measured.”

This avoids a familiar error: selecting B because it wins one column, then
retroactively describing solubility as secondary. The same table also has no
evidence on exposure, organismal effect, or clinical benefit.

## Alternatives, limits, and an independent exercise

A weighted utility `U = Σ w_j z_j` can be useful only when each normalized
`z_j`, direction, weight `w_j`, and missing-value rule were fixed before
selection. A Pareto front retains candidates for which no other candidate is at
least as good on every declared endpoint and better on one. Both methods conceal
missing measurements unless missingness is shown explicitly. Neither replaces a
TPP gate or independent validation.

**Handworked exercise (not runnable or empirical):** Use the same constructed
TPP. Analogue D has inhibition at 1 µM of `80%`, `pIC50 = 6.55`, solubility
proxy `24`, and reporter inhibition `22%`; E has inhibition at 1 µM of `65%`,
`pIC50 = 6.26`, solubility proxy `29`, and reporter inhibition `4%`. Which candidates advance under the fragment? Relative to A,
calculate D's `ΔpIC50` and its constructed `IC50` ratio. Name one next
measurement for a C-versus-D choice and one conclusion the table cannot support.

<details data-worked-answer>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

D fails the less-than-15% reporter gate despite meeting its other listed values,
including the constructed 1 µM inhibition gate; E meets the constructed 1 µM
inhibition gate but misses the preferred `pIC50`, which was not declared a minimum, so it meets
this fragment and can remain a comparator. D has `ΔpIC50 = 6.55 − 6.10 = +0.45`,
so its constructed `IC50` is `10^0.45 = 2.82`-fold lower than A's under matched
fits. An orthogonal target-proximal readout or an independently prepared repeat
could distinguish a persistent biological series signal from a measurement
artifact. This table cannot establish target engagement in a relevant organism,
human exposure, safety, or clinical benefit.
