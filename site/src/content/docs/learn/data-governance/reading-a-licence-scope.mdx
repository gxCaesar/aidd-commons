## Learning goals and prerequisites

You will read seven licence scopes written by people who had to be precise
about what they were granting, and you will find that the licence *name* is
the least informative part of the grant. You will practise taking a scope
apart into four questions, and you will apply that to a record in this
catalogue. Read [Data contracts and licences](/learn/data-governance/data-contracts/)
and [Entity resolution, scientific lineage and permitted
reuse](/learn/data-governance/identity-lineage-and-reuse/) first; the
earlier lesson establishes that access is not permission, and this one is
about the mechanics of the permission itself. This is a reading lesson: no
licence here is a legal opinion, and no reader is advised to rely on one.

## The four questions a scope has to answer

A licence name is a label. A scope is a set of answers, and a reader who wants
to know what they may do has to find four of them.

**Which asset does the grant cover?** A dataset, its code, an article and a
hosted service are four different assets and they are often under four
different terms in the same place. "CC BY 4.0" on an article says nothing
about the repository next to it.

**Which use does it permit?** Research, commercial use, redistribution and the
making of derived products are four different permissions. Several of the
scopes below draw that distinction explicitly.

**What does it withhold?** A grant can be open and still withhold something:
the parts of a dataset that came from a third party, a version that predates
the licence, or a service behind a download button.

**What does it leave unresolved?** The scope of a permissive grant often stops
where the authors' own rights stop. A scope that says so is more useful to a
reader than one that says nothing.

## Seven scopes, read

**FreeSolv v0.52.** The repository's current branch states that the data are
covered by a licence file specifying CC-BY 4.0 and the code by a separate MIT
file, and the same statement limits the grant: to the extent the data are the
authors' own to license, and not for the parts that carry an upstream
restriction. The pinned v0.52 tag the record names carries neither licence
file nor a licence section, so the record carries no licence at all for the
version it names. Two lessons sit in that one scope: a licence on a branch is
not a licence on a tag, and a grant with a scope limit is narrower than its
name.

**GTEx Portal.** The portal publishes a licence of its own. It charges no
fees or royalties and it restricts no use, and it requires two things: that a
user acknowledge the portal as the source with a stated phrase, and that a
user who republishes the data either keep the most current version or make
known that the product does not reflect the most current data. A bespoke
licence is still a licence, and it can impose duties that are not
restrictions. It also reserves the right to change the format of the
machine-readable data, which is a change a reader's pipeline has to survive.

**ToxCast.** The agency's own statement is that its computational toxicology
data are open data, free of all copyright restrictions, and fully and freely
available for both non-commercial and commercial use. Nothing is withheld
because there is nothing to withhold: these are works of the United States
government. A record that leaves the licence field empty for such a resource
has failed to say something true, and a record that writes "public domain"
where the source says "open data" has said something the source did not.

**SAbDab2.** The machine-learning release states its own terms, on the
release, so the record can point at the exact artefact and version. That is the
cleanest case here, and it is worth noticing what it takes: a deposit that
states its licence, a version number, and a description of what is in it.

**PLABench.** The code is MIT, and the same sentence says the grant covers
the code and not the third-party model weights or the benchmark data, which
keep their own terms; the repository records the official URL, checksum and
licence of every third-party checkpoint it fetches. The data deposit carries
only the Zenodo identifier other-open, with no licence name and no rights
text, so the deposit does not itself name a grant. A permissive code licence
does not travel to the data beside it, and a deposit that says only "open"
does not say what the openness consists of.

**Beyond Affinity.** The article carries CC BY 4.0 on arXiv, and the
benchmark's code repository carries no licence file at all. The repository is
public and readable, and a reader who assumes the article's licence reaches
the code is wrong about the one thing they would rely on.

**DepMap.** Current portal data are freely available for internal or
non-profit research, and commercial use is not permitted under these terms
and may require a separate licence agreement from the rights-holders. The
clause worth noticing is the one about *models trained on* the data: a
researcher who trains a model on free-for-research data and then uses the
model commercially has not stayed inside the research permission, and a
licence that only said "free for research" would not have told them so.

## Worked case

A reader wants to redistribute the SAbDab2 structures inside a teaching
package. The record's scope says the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 licence
covers the machine-learning release, and the release states it for itself.
So the four questions have answers: the asset is the release as deposited;
the use permitted is redistribution with attribution; what is withheld is
whatever the upstream structures carry, which the release does not state; and
what is unresolved is whether the individual structures can be
redistributed at all, because the deposit's grant covers the curation and the
release rather than every upstream structure. The reader can proceed with
attribution and a visible provenance note, and must not represent the
package as clearing every upstream right.

## Independent exercise

**Handworked exercise (not runnable or empirical):** You are given four
records. The first states that the data are covered by a licence file
specifying CC-BY 4.0, that the grant reaches the data only to the extent
they are the authors' own to license, and that the version you pinned carries
no licence file at all. The second states that its data are free of all
copyright restrictions and freely available for both non-commercial and
commercial use, and that its repository carries no licence file. The third
states that the code is MIT and that the third-party weights and the data
are excluded from that grant, and that the data deposit carries only the
identifier other-open. The fourth states that the data are free for internal
or non-profit research, that commercial use is not permitted, and that this
includes the sale or product use of models trained on the data. (a) For each
record, name which asset the grant covers, which use it permits, what it
withholds, and what it leaves unresolved. (b) Which of the four grants would
let you put the data inside a commercial product, and under what conditions?
(c) Which one looks most open by name and is narrowest in fact, and why does
the name mislead? (d) For the record whose code is MIT and whose data
deposit says only other-open, what would you have to obtain before shipping
the data?

<details data-worked-answer>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

(a) The first covers the repository's current branch, not the pinned version,
so the asset you hold is not covered; it permits whatever the authors can
license and withholds the parts carrying upstream restrictions, and it
leaves the pinned version's terms unresolved. The second covers the data as
such, permits commercial and non-commercial use, withholds nothing because
the work is not copyrightable, and leaves nothing unresolved on its face. The
third covers the code, permits reuse and redistribution of that code,
withholds the third-party weights and the data, and leaves unresolved what
"other-open" consists of, because no licence name and no rights text are
attached. The fourth covers the current portal data, permits internal and
non-profit research, withholds commercial use including the sale or product
use of models trained on the data, and leaves unresolved whether a separate
agreement will be granted and on what terms.

(b) The second, on its face, because it says free for both non-commercial and
commercial use. The fourth permits it only for internal or non-profit use
and requires a separate agreement for anything commercial, and the third
permits nothing about the data at all. The first permits nothing about the
pinned version, because the version carries no licence.

(c) The third, by name: MIT is the most permissive licence in this set, and
it is the narrowest in fact for the data, because it covers code and
excludes the data and the weights, while the deposit that holds the data
states no licence name at all. The name misleads because a reader who reads
the repository's licence badge has read a fact about the code and is likely
to remember it as a fact about the project.

(d) A grant that names what the deposit actually provides. The Zenodo
identifier other-open with no name and no rights text is not a licence, and
a reader who needs to ship the data has to ask the depositors what the
identifier covers, and separately has to establish the terms of every
third-party checkpoint the benchmark fetches, because the MIT grant excludes
those and the repository only records their URLs, checksums and licences in
its own inventory.

</details>
