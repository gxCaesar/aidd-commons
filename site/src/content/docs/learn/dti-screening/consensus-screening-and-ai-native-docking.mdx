## Learning goals and prerequisites

You will read a virtual screening platform's own evaluation across three
benchmarks and notice that the choice of benchmark moved its headline number
by more than an order of magnitude, and that the platform's own authors say
so. You will separate three claims that are usually reported as one: how many
components a platform offers, how well the platform does on a benchmark, and
whether a setting chosen on one target transfers to another. You will then
read an AI-native docking framework's abstract and mark, claim by claim, which
parts are a measurement and which are a demonstration. Read [Screening and
affinity](/learn/dti-screening/screening-and-affinity/), [Screening
prevalence, affinity scales, and transfer](/learn/dti-screening/screening-prevalence-and-transfer/)
and [Docking, pose generation, and physical validity](/learn/dti-screening/docking-and-pose-validity/)
first. This is a reading and hand-arithmetic lesson: this project ran no
docking and measured no enrichment of its own.

## Why a consensus exists, and what it costs the reader

Consensus virtual screening runs the same compound library through several
docking engines, keeps poses by several selection rules, rescores them with
several scoring functions, and combines the resulting rankings by several
consensus rules. The motivation is variance reduction. No single engine's
ranking is reliable across targets, and a ranking averaged over several
engines is usually more stable than any one of them.

The cost is that the space of settings is combinatorial, and the reader has to
choose one point in it. The platform read in this lesson, DockM8, states in
its own paper that it supports five docking engines, 17 pose-selection
methods, 17 scoring functions and five consensus methods, and that it yields
over 55 million configurable protocols. The product of those four headline
counts is 5 × 17 × 17 × 5 = 7,225, which is not 55 million. So the paper's
protocol count is describing a larger space than the four categories it
names, most plausibly by counting configurable input conformers, ligand
preparation and grid settings as well. The number is the paper's, and this
lesson does not reconstruct it; the observation worth carrying is that a
platform's "breadth" figure and its "number of engines" figure are different
numbers and only the second one is checkable by hand.

The second framework read here, TRIDS, takes a different route to the same
problem. Its abstract describes an AI-native docking framework that guides
conformation sampling with the gradient of an analytic scoring function.
That changes the failure mode rather than the aim. In a conventional pipeline
the search explores and the scoring function ranks what the search found; an
error in the scoring function then only misranks candidates. When the
scoring function's gradient steers the sampling, the same error steers the
search, and the search commits to it. A framework of this kind is not a
consensus of engines but a single search with a differentiable objective, and
its accuracy and its physical validity are both properties of that objective.

## Worked case: one platform, three benchmarks, and the authors' own conclusion

The DockM8 paper is by Lacour, Agha, Hirsch and Volkamer, published in
*Journal of Cheminformatics* volume 18 on 18 September 2026 under DOI
10.1186/s13321-026-01287-2. Its abstract reports a systematic evaluation on
DEKOIS 2.0, DUD-E and Lit-PCBA, and gives median relative enrichments of
100%, 82.59% and 8.85% respectively. Those are the paper's own figures and
not a measurement this project made.

The spread is the finding. The same platform, over the same protocol family,
moves from a median relative enrichment of 100% on one benchmark to 8.85% on
another. Whatever the absolute scale of that metric, the ratio between the
best and the worst benchmark is a little over eleven, so the benchmark a number
was measured on is part of what the number means. A reader who takes the DEKOIS
2.0 number to a target whose binding mode is
driven by lipophilicity rather than by a buried pocket is using the number
the paper itself warns against, because the paper's own conclusion is that no
pose-selection or consensus strategy excels universally and that workflows
have to be tailored to the target.

Two things about the paper's framing are worth reading carefully rather than
parroting. First, it describes DockM8 as, in its own words, the most
comprehensive open-source platform to integrate the complete consensus
structure-based virtual screening pipeline. That is a self-characterisation
of scope, published by the authors of the platform, and this project does not
turn it into an independent comparison of platforms; no such comparison was
run here. Second, the paper's own sentence that no strategy universally
excels is a stronger and more useful claim than the headline enrichment
would be, because it is the part that survives the change of benchmark. A
platform claim that survives every benchmark it was tested on is the stronger
claim, and this one does not make it.

TRIDS is a useful contrast because its abstract is much lighter on numbers. It
states that a single docking task completes in a fraction of a second, that
the GPU memory footprint is a few hundred megabytes, that the method improves
docking accuracy and passes physical validation, and that it was applied to a
high-throughput TNF-α virtual screening that produced hits with novel
scaffolds. The abstract names no benchmark and reports no metric value. So
its evidence in the abstract is a demonstration on one target rather than a
comparison against other methods on shared test sets, and the two kinds of
abstract support two different amounts of belief. The arXiv record
2510.24186 also identifies DSDP as the predecessor framework, with DSDPFlex
alongside it in the repository's reference list, so this is the third
generation of a line of work rather than a new approach from nothing.

The licence layers differ too, and the difference is not cosmetic. DockM8's
repository carries a GNU General Public Licence 3.0 file, which is copyleft
and binds derivative redistribution; the licences of the docking engines and
scoring functions it calls are their own and are not settled by the
platform's licence. TRIDS's repository carries the Apache License, Version
2.0, which is permissive, and its README adds a separate statement that the
software is basically free to academic users and that anyone who wishes to
sell it or use it in a commercial product should contact the authors. Those
are two different kinds of fact about the same repository: what the licence
file grants, and what the authors say they will agree to. A reader who needs
commercial rights has to answer both.

## Alternatives, limits, and independent exercise

The limits of what this lesson establishes are specific. This project ran no
docking, so no number above is a measurement here. The three enrichments are
median relative enrichments as the paper defines them; this lesson does not
reconstruct that metric's denominator, and a relative enrichment is a
comparison against a decoy or a baseline rather than a binding measurement.
DEKOIS 2.0 and DUD-E are decoy-based benchmarks, and enrichment on decoys is
evidence about ranking actives above decoys, not evidence that anything
binds. And a consensus inherits every engine's systematic error as well as
its variance: averaging rankings over engines that share a scoring
function's blind spot does not remove the blind spot.

**Handworked exercise (not runnable or empirical):** A platform's abstract
states that it supports five docking engines, 17 pose-selection methods, 17
scoring functions and five consensus methods, that it yields over 55 million
configurable protocols, and that it achieves median relative enrichments of
100%, 82.59% and 8.85% on three benchmarks. It concludes that no
pose-selection or consensus strategy universally excels. (a) Compute the
product of the four component counts and state what it is not equal to, and
what a reader can conclude about the space of protocols from that. (b) By
what factor does the reported median relative enrichment differ between its
best and its worst benchmark, and what does that factor say about how much
of the platform's headline performance is a property of the benchmark rather
than of the platform? (c) The abstract's conclusion and the headline
enrichment point in opposite directions. Which of the two would you keep if
you had to defend a screening decision for a target unlike all three
benchmarks, and why? (d) Name one measurement this project would have to run
before it could state a consensus screening claim as its own rather than as
the paper's.

<details data-worked-answer>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

(a) 5 × 17 × 17 × 5 = 7,225. It is not 55 million, so the protocol count
describes a larger space than the four categories the abstract names, most
plausibly by including configurable input conformers, ligand preparation and
grid settings. From the four counts a reader can check the second figure's
order of magnitude and nothing finer. (b) 100 / 8.85 ≈ 11.3, so the benchmark
choice moves the reported number by about a factor of eleven, which is
larger than any plausible gap between two of the platform's own scoring
functions. Most of the apparent performance is a property of the benchmark.
(c) Keep the conclusion, not the headline. The headline is a property of one
benchmark and one protocol family, and the paper's own conclusion says the
ranking is not stable across targets, so a number from one benchmark cannot
be defended for an unlike target. The conclusion is the claim that survives
the change of benchmark, and it is also the one that tells the reader what to
do: pick the setting per target and measure it there. (d) A decision here
would have to fix a target, a library, a protocol setting chosen in advance
rather than after seeing the answer, and an enrichment metric with a stated
denominator, run against at least one other setting on the same target and
the same library. Until such a measurement exists, the platform's numbers
are the paper's, and the honest statement is that the benchmark moves the
number more than the platform does.

</details>
