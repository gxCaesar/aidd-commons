## Learning goals and prerequisites

A classical force field fixes the functional form of an energy surface and fits
its parameters. A machine-learned interatomic potential lets the model learn the
surface from data. That substitution is the whole idea, and it is also the whole
difficulty: the classical force field states in its own documentation which
terms it contains and what was fitted to what, while a learned surface inherits
the reach of whatever corpus produced it, and that corpus is often the part a
reader cannot inspect.

Read this after [force fields and sampling](../force-fields-and-sampling/), which
fixes the classical contract, and after
[physical model and system state](../physical-model-system-state/), which fixes
what a system state is and why frames of one trajectory are not independent
observations.

By the end you should be able to state, for any published interatomic
potential, whether a reader can run the released code, retrain the released
model, and inspect the corpus the model was trained on. These are three
different questions and the open artefacts answer them differently.

## From a fixed functional form to a learned surface

A fixed force field states its functional form in advance. A reader can read the
equation, count the terms, and see which physical effects were left out on
purpose. Nothing about the field is hidden by the data.

A learned potential replaces that equation with a model whose behaviour on an
unseen configuration is determined by the data it saw. The model is usually
described as a graph network over atoms with a stated equivariance property,
and the equivariance is a real and checkable property of the architecture. What
the model does with a chemistry outside its training distribution is not a
property of the architecture at all. It is a property of the corpus, and the
corpus is exactly the part these releases vary most in how they document it.

The consequence for a reader is a change of what must be checked. Asking
whether a force field is a good approximation means asking whether its terms
are adequate. Asking whether a learned potential is a good approximation means
asking whether its corpus contains the chemistry and whether the corpus can be
inspected.

## What the six released code bases are

Six open releases are registered in the atlas for this track. They are not
ranked here, because the records do not carry a comparison under a shared
protocol. What each one is, taken from its own record, is this.

**NequIP** is a code base for E(3)-equivariant machine-learned interatomic
potentials that also distributes pretrained foundation potentials through its own
model host. **MACE** is a code base for machine-learned potentials with
higher-order equivariant message passing, publishing pretrained potentials for
materials and molecules, with an academic source licence on a subset of them.
**CHGNet** is a pretrained graph neural network potential for charge-informed
atomistic modelling, trained on Materials Project relaxation trajectories and
used to predict energy, force, stress and magnetisation. **MatterSim** is a deep
learning atomistic model used as a machine-learning force field across the
periodic table, released with two pretrained checkpoints, a finetuning script
and a model card. **SevenNet** is a graph neural network interatomic potential
package with pretrained universal potentials and a fine-tuning interface, usable
through the ASE calculator and LAMMPS. **TorchANI** is a PyTorch library for
building, training and running ANI-style neural network interatomic potentials,
with pretrained ANI-2x weights published on the Hugging Face Hub.

Each of the six is a real, inspectable artefact with a source licence: MIT for
NequIP, MACE, MatterSim and TorchANI, BSD-3-Clause for CHGNet, and MIT for
SevenNet. Four of the six additionally publish weights under a licence the
record names, and in two cases the weights sit under a different and more
restrictive licence than the code.

## The openness picture across the six

The atlas keeps seven openness dimensions apart for exactly this reason. Read
across the six records and the pattern is clear.

| Dimension | Available | Unverified |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Inference code | 6 | 0 |
| Training code | 6 | 0 |
| Architecture | 6 | 0 |
| Weights | 4 | 2 |
| Training data | 2 | 4 |
| Data recipe | 0 | 6 |
| Evaluation code | 1 | 5 |

Inference, training and architecture are open everywhere. Weights are open in
four of the six, and the two exceptions are CHGNet and SevenNet, whose records
state that no opened page gives a licence for the published checkpoints even
though the repositories ship a licence for the code beside them.

The bottom two rows are the ones a reader cannot infer. The data recipe is
unverified in all six records. The evaluation code is open in one, MACE, and
unverified in the other five. A repository can contain the scripts that trained
a model without containing a record of how the training set was assembled.

## Where the training corpus stays dark

Training data is open in two of the six records. MACE's record supports the
claim for the MACE-MP checkpoints, whose training corpus the README names as
MPtrj selected from Materials Project relaxation trajectories. CHGNet's record
supports it for the MPtrj corpus, which the model card records with a 9-0.5-0.5
train, validation and test split.

The other four leave it unverified, and the reasons differ in a way that
matters. NequIP's foundation potentials are pre-trained on OMat24 and
fine-tuned on a subsample of Alexandria and on MPtrj, and no opened page states
the redistribution term for that subsample. MatterSim's README describes the
corpus only as data generated through the workflows introduced in its own
paper, and the model card names a test set drawn from MPtrj and from Alexandria
without giving redistribution terms for the Alexandria subset. SevenNet's
documentation names 15 open ab initio datasets by task labels such as mpa,
matpes_r2scan, omol25_low, oc20, oc22 and odac23, which names a selection
without describing how the selection was made. TorchANI's register of built-in
datasets records the DOI and article for the ANI-2x corpus as TODO.

The Zenodo record behind the NequIP MP models states that those models train
solely on MPtrj of about 1.5 million frames and are recommended for
benchmarking rather than production work. That is a useful statement about a
corpus size. It is not a statement about which chemistry the 1.5 million frames
contain.

## When one name means two things

Two assets share the OMat24 name, and they carry different licences. On the
Hugging Face Hub the OMat24 dataset is tagged cc-by-4.0. The OMat24 checkpoint
collection published by the same organisation is tagged other and carries a
bespoke OMat24 License that incorporates the FAIR Acceptable Use Policy.

This is not a pedantic distinction. A reader who sees "OMat24" in a training
description cannot tell from the name which of the two assets is meant, and the
two have different terms. The NequIP and SevenNet records both note it, and
both also note that the fine-tuning corpus is a subsample of Alexandria whose
redistribution term no opened page states.

Two more names carry a similar hazard. The MACE README names the OMAT, OMOL,
MATPES and SPICE corpora for its non-MP checkpoints without stating their
licences. And MatterSim offers more advanced pretrained versions and further
materials capabilities through a hosted product, whose pages state no licence.

## When two results are not independent

SevenNet's own README states that its core model is based on NequIP. Results
attributed to SevenNet and results attributed to NequIP therefore do not
measure two independent architectures, and a comparison that reads them as two
independent entries in a leaderboard is reading a family tree as a sample.

The same applies to the fine-tuning corpora. When one architecture is
fine-tuned on a corpus another was pre-trained on, the two are not independent
with respect to the chemistry they cover, whatever their architectures.

## What a reported error does and does not mean

The records are careful about the numbers their own pages report, and the
cautions are worth carrying into any comparison.

The CHGNet model card reports mean absolute errors for energy, force, stress
and magnetisation on its MPtrj test split, and the record notes that the
publisher page for the article was not opened during the review, so the
citation is recorded as the README states it and not from the article itself.
The MACE README states that the MACE-MP models are trained on MPtrj raw DFT
energies from VASP outputs and are not directly comparable to Materials
Project or CHGNet energies, which carry MP2020Compatibility corrections. The
SevenNet documentation reports F1 and RMSD on the WBM dataset together with a
combined performance score, but the opened pages give neither the metric
definitions nor the evaluation code.

Two failures of the same metric are therefore not two measurements. The labels
differ, and the record that says so is the same record that says the
evaluation code is unverified in five of the six cases. An error quoted from a
model card is a number whose protocol the reader may not be able to obtain.

## What a matched comparison would need

A comparison that ranks these six would need three things the records do not
currently carry. It would need one evaluation code path that every model is
run through, since five of the six leave it unverified. It would need one
stated split and one stated metric definition, since the reported errors use
different corpora, different labels and, in the case of the combined
performance score, an undefined aggregation. And it would need the training
corpus terms, because a model whose corpus cannot be redistributed cannot be
retrained by a reader who is not already inside the rights holder.

What the records do support today is a statement about what each artefact
publishes. That is a different statement from a ranking, and it is the one a
reader can actually act on.

## Worked case

A reader wants to know whether the NequIP foundation potentials can be
reproduced by someone outside the project. The record answers in three
separate parts.

The code can be obtained. The inference code, the training code and the
architecture are all available, and the NequIP Python package is under MIT.

The weights can be obtained. The foundation potential packages listed on the
model host carry CC-BY-4.0, and the Zenodo record gives the model artifact a
DOI. One detail is worth carrying forward, because it shows what a licence
field does not say: the model host listing gives one licence field for the
model package and does not separate code terms from weight terms, so the MIT
terms recorded in the atlas come from the repository licence file rather than
from the listing.

The training cannot be reproduced. The data recipe is unverified, and the
corpus is described as OMat24 with fine-tuning on a subsample of Alexandria
and on MPtrj, where no opened page states the redistribution term for that
subsample. The Zenodo record names the frame count and says the models are
recommended for benchmarking, which tells a reader how much data there is and
not what is in it.

So the answer to the reader's question is that the released potentials can be
run and inspected, and that the training that produced them cannot be
repeated from what is published.

## Independent exercise

Consider the six records above and answer these three questions, then compare
with the table in the openness section.

1. How many of the six let a reader re-run the training, meaning that a training
   script and the inputs it consumed are both present? Give the count for the
   training code alone and for the data recipe alone, and state what the
   difference between the two counts means for a reader who wants to retrain
   rather than merely to run.
2. Four of the six publish weights under a licence their record names, and two
   do not. Name the two, and say what a licence record tells you and what it
   does not.
3. Two records name a corpus whose redistribution term no opened page states.
   Name the corpus and the two records. Now say whether a reader who cannot
   obtain that corpus can still use the released weights, and what they lose.

<summary>Worked answer</summary>

1. Training code is available in 6 of 6. The data recipe is unverified in 6 of
   6, so the count for the training inputs is 0. The difference is the whole
   point: running the training script is not the same as reproducing the
   training, because a script that has been run once on a corpus nobody can
   obtain is a script whose output cannot be checked. A reader who wants to
   retrain has the code and, in two cases, the corpus, and in the other four
   has the code and a name.
2. The two are CHGNet and SevenNet. A licence record tells you the terms under
   which the artefact may be used and redistributed. It does not tell you
   whether the artefact exists in a form that can be obtained, whether the
   weights are the ones the paper used, or whether the corpus behind them can
   be reached.
3. The corpus is the Alexandria subsample, named by the NequIP and SevenNet
   records, and the terms of the second OMat24 asset are the other case. A
   reader who cannot obtain the corpus can still use the released weights,
   because the weights are published under their own licence and the corpus is
   not needed to evaluate them. What they lose is the ability to retrain, to
   fine-tune on a related chemistry, or to establish how far the weights
   extrapolate, since that judgement is a statement about the corpus.

## What this material does not show

This reading does not compare the six releases, and it does not rank them. The
records carry no evaluation under a shared protocol, so any ordering would be
an artefact of which page happened to be read first.

It does not establish that a learned potential is accurate, or that it is more
accurate than a force field. It reports what each release publishes and what
each leaves unverified.

It does not establish that the training corpora are small, biased or
well-balanced. The one corpus whose size the records state is about 1.5 million
frames, and a frame count is not a description of chemistry.

It does not tell you that a weight is unusable because its licence is
unstated. It tells you that the terms are unknown, which is a different
problem with a different remedy.
