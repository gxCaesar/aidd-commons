when a reported barrier is sensitive to that partition. Read [Force fields,
solvent, and sampling](/learn/physics-based-modeling/force-fields-and-sampling/)
and [Free-energy cycles, endpoints, and convergence](/learn/physics-based-modeling/free-energy-cycles-and-convergence/)
first. This lesson is conceptual and handworked. It supplies neither a reaction
procedure nor a calculated mechanism for a real molecular system.

## Why split a system into QM and MM regions?

Classical molecular mechanics can efficiently represent many bonded and
nonbonded motions, but its fixed atom types and charges generally cannot describe
an event in which electronic structure changes. Bond formation or cleavage,
charge redistribution, change of electronic state, and some metal-centred
processes are common reasons to ask a quantum-mechanical question. Applying QM
to every atom can be expensive, whereas applying MM to the changing chemical
region can make the question ill posed.

QM/MM divides an **entire system** into a primary QM subsystem `Q` and an MM
subsystem `M`. A useful schematic energy is
`E_QM/MM = E_QM(Q) + E_MM(M) + E_int(Q,M)`.
The interaction term is not optional bookkeeping: it determines how the local
electronic problem feels steric and electrostatic surroundings. The original
Warshel and Levitt study explicitly considered a substrate, enzyme and surrounding
solvent while separating quantum and classical energy factors
[Warshel and Levitt](https://weizmann.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/theoretical-studies-of-enzymic-reactions-dielectric-electrostatic/).

The split is a modelling hypothesis. It should be selected from the chemistry
of the declared event, not from a desire to obtain a smaller atom count. Include
atoms whose bonds, formal charge, protonation, coordination, or electronic state
are expected to change, plus nearby groups needed to represent direct electronic
or steric coupling. Keep more distant surroundings in `M` when their principal
role is environmental. The correct boundary is assessed by sensitivity tests,
not guessed from a residue label alone.

| Choice           | What to specify                                                            | Why it changes the result                                           |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| QM region `Q`    | atom list, charge, spin, electronic method and basis                       | electronic rearrangement is only possible inside the defined region |
| MM region `M`    | force field, protonation, solvent/ions, restraints and boundary conditions | environment can stabilize geometries and charge distributions       |
| embedding        | mechanical, electrostatic, or another named coupling scheme                | it determines how `M` acts on `Q`                                   |
| cut bonds        | capping/link treatment, charge handling and atom identities                | artificial boundary terms can perturb energies                      |
| event coordinate | geometric/electronic collective variables and reference states             | a barrier only has meaning along a named path                       |

## Boundary treatment is part of the hypothesis

A covalent boundary can leave an open valence in the QM calculation. A common
repair uses a link atom or other capping construction, but its position and the
treatment of nearby MM charges are assumptions that can affect the result.
Mechanical embedding evaluates QM and MM pieces with a classical-style coupling.
Electrostatic embedding lets MM charges enter the QM Hamiltonian, so the QM
electron density responds to the environment. Neither label makes an answer
universally reliable.

The Lin and Truhlar review writes the same partition as a primary system,
secondary system, and their interaction, and distinguishes mechanical from
electrostatic embedding and several boundary treatments
[Lin and Truhlar](https://comp.chem.umn.edu/qmmmreview/QMMMreview05.htm).
Its central practical lesson is still current: report where the boundary lies,
what it cuts, and how its effects were tested. A calculation that changes
substantially when one adjacent group crosses the boundary has discovered an
uncertainty, not a mechanism conclusion.

## A reactive coordinate must be named

“The reaction occurred” is not a numerical endpoint. A simple constructed
coordinate might be `xi(x) = r_form - r_break`, where `r_form` and `r_break`
are two declared interatomic distances. It can locate a path hypothesis, but it
may fail to describe proton transfer, solvent rearrangement, alternative
conformations, or a change of spin state. The reported quantities must say
whether they are optimized-path energies, sampled free energies, or another
defined object. A transition-like geometry from a short optimization is not by
itself a rate prediction.

For any result, retain the entire-system coordinates, QM atom list and charge,
electronic method, MM model, embedding, boundary treatment, restraint and
sampling protocol, reaction-coordinate definition, and reference state. This
record makes it possible to tell an informative QM/MM sensitivity analysis from
a diagram with a coloured active site.

## Worked case: a boundary sensitivity finding

All values in this case are **constructed for teaching**. A team defines a
reaction-coordinate hypothesis `xi(x) = r_form - r_break`. Its first partition
`Q_24` contains `24` atoms and gives an optimized-path barrier of
`17.4 kcal/mol`. A second partition `Q_38` adds one nearby charged group and
its covalent neighbour, uses the same named electronic method and environment,
and gives `19.1 kcal/mol`. The difference is `19.1 - 17.4 = 1.7 kcal/mol`.

The defensible conclusion is that this constructed barrier is boundary-sensitive
at least across `Q_24` and `Q_38`. It is not that `19.1 kcal/mol` is the true
barrier, that the added group “causes catalysis,” or that either calculation
predicts a measured rate. The next useful work is to inspect the charge,
geometry, and boundary treatment around the changed group; if the scientific
decision depends on less than `1.7 kcal/mol`, the partition uncertainty is
already decision-relevant.

## Limits and independent exercise

QM/MM can omit relevant states, use an inadequate electronic method, or sample
the surrounding environment poorly. Enlarging `Q` may reduce one boundary error
while increasing cost enough to reduce sampling. Agreement between two choices
does not remove shared assumptions. Report a negative sensitivity result as a
result; do not hide it by selecting the partition with the more convenient
number.

**Handworked exercise (not runnable or empirical):** A constructed event uses
`Q_small = 18` atoms and `Q_large = 32` atoms. The same stated path gives
`DeltaE‡ = 12.0 kcal/mol` and `DeltaE‡ = 13.8 kcal/mol`, respectively. Compute
the change. Name two records needed for another reader to interpret it, and write
one claim that would exceed the evidence.

<details data-worked-answer>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

The change is `13.8 - 12.0 = 1.8 kcal/mol`. Needed records include the atom
lists, charge/spin and electronic method for `Q`, the MM force field and
environment, embedding and capping treatment, complete coordinates, restraints,
and the definition of `DeltaE‡`. The result supports a boundary-sensitivity
observation under the constructed protocol. It does not establish an experimental
activation free energy, a reaction rate, or a causal role for the added atoms.

</details>
